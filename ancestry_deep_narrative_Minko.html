Seven layers follow, each deeper than the last. First, the whole excavation in one pass.

Set against people alive today, Minko’s DNA lands closest to Romanians (0.018) and Bulgarians (0.021), with Gagauz, Montenegrins, Moldovans, Serbs and Macedonians right behind. That is a neighborhood, not a single country, and his DNA sits near the middle of it. The MyHeritage labels (Greek and Albanian about 47%, Balkan about 38%) are two windows onto this same Southeast European gene pool, not separate ancestors.

Among ancient skeletons, the nearest are from medieval Macedonia (0.030) and medieval Bulgaria (0.034). Those are the centuries when Slavic-speaking newcomers mixed with the people already living in the Balkans. A 2023 ancient-DNA study estimated that Slavic-related ancestry makes up roughly 30–60% of present-day Balkan peoples, so a medieval Balkan profile is just what the history predicts.

Under the medieval layer, the models read the genome as a blend of two big ingredients: steppe herders from north of the Black Sea (about 52–56%) and early farmers from Anatolia (about 42–46%). Hunter-gatherers from Mesolithic Europe show up on their own at only about 2%, though more of their ancestry rides inside the steppe share, which had already absorbed hunters before it reached the Balkans.

The raw file backs up the European picture: two copies of the light-skin variant at both SLC24A5 and SLC45A2, two copies of the HERC2 variant most strongly linked to blue eyes, and one copy of the lactase-persistence variant. The East Asian–associated markers checked (EDAR, ABCC11, ALDH2, OCA2) all show their ancestral versions, so none of them hints at East Asian ancestry.

The label on the box is the least interesting part

The test says: 47% Greek and Albanian, 38% Balkan, 9% East European, 5% South Italian. That doesn’t mean Minko has a Greek grandmother or an Italian great-grandfather. The test compares his DNA with people alive today and reports who he resembles. “Greek and Albanian” really means “resembles the people who carry the old Mediterranean farming heritage,” a heritage shared by almost everyone in southeastern Europe.

Run the same DNA through a different test and the labels change. The DNA doesn’t. So the labels get set aside, and the dig begins.

What the Labels Are Telling Us, and What They Are Not

Every dig starts at the surface. Here the surface is the ethnicity estimate a standard DNA test hands back, and it is the least reliable part of the whole report.

MyHeritage’s ethnicity estimate for this kit:

South Italian4.9%
Baltic1.1%

MyHeritage also flags three Additional Genetic Groups at lower confidence, finer-grained signals the main breakdown has no room for: Bulgaria, Romania, and Bosnia and Herzegovina.

These percentages are not ancestry fractions in the way a family tree would show them. They come from an algorithm that compares DNA against modern reference populations and asks which living groups it most resembles. A different algorithm with a different reference panel gives different percentages, as the other calculators in this report show. What matters is what stays consistent across all of them. Here is how the five labels read:

Greek and Albanian (46.8%) does not mean a Greek or Albanian grandparent. About half of the profile resembles those living populations, which preserve a lot of the old Aegean and Anatolian farming heritage that also underlies the ancient Thracians, Dacians and Illyrians. Greeks and Albanians carry relatively less of the Slavic-era signal, so the algorithm uses them as the nearest stand-in for the older Balkan layer.

Balkan (38.4%) is the algorithm’s window onto Slavic-era and local Balkan ancestry together: people already in the Balkans before the 6th century, plus the migrants who arrived after.

East European (8.8%) is ancestry shared with Slavic-speaking and Baltic-region populations to the north, the kind carried south by the early medieval migrations. It is not a separate East European ancestor.

South Italian (4.9%) is not Italian ancestry in a family-tree sense. Southern Italians and Balkan Greeks both draw heavily on Anatolian farmers plus later eastern-Mediterranean ancestry, so this slice is the same farming signal wearing a different label.

Baltic (1.1%) and the additional groups MyHeritage notes are what the algorithm reports when a genome sits at the center of a continuum and resembles all its neighbors at once.

The Bottom Line Read properly, the labels describe one position in one place: the Eastern Balkans, where early farmers, steppe herders and the older peoples of the Balkans were folded together. The percentages are the algorithm’s way of describing that position with the reference groups it happens to have, not a count of ethnic ancestors.
Takeaway The label is not the finding. Country names are modern reference tags, not a family tree.

The Slavs arrive, and mix with the people already here

Between the 6th and 9th centuries, Slavic-speaking families moved south into the Balkans. They didn’t replace the people already there. They mixed with the descendants of Thracians, Illyrians and Romanized provincials, alongside settlers from Anatolia who had arrived under Roman and Byzantine rule, and from that blend the medieval Bulgarians emerged. Ancient DNA shows the scale: Slavic-related ancestry makes up roughly 30–60% of present-day Balkan peoples.

Minko’s DNA is closest to skeletons from medieval Macedonia and Bulgaria. Avar-period Hungary is a mixed picture. Some commoner groups from the Carpathian Basin sit just as close to him, while groups carrying East Asian steppe ancestry, above all the ruling-class graves, are far away. His DNA reflects the European-type population of that world, not the steppe aristocracy that governed the plain next door.

If a guess is needed for the mix: roughly a third Slavic-related, about half older Balkan peoples, and a fifth Anatolian arrivals from the Roman and Byzantine era. Treat those fractions as a guess, not a measurement.

The Medieval Stratum: Bulgaria in Deep Time

The living-population map shows where the genome sits today. To see how that position formed, the dig removes the modern layer and goes down to the Middle Ages.

On the consensus coordinate, the nearest medieval references are Macedonia Medieval (0.030) and Bulgaria Medieval (0.034). Both are averages of real genomes from Eastern Balkan graves. A distance near 0.03 means Minko’s modeled coordinate sits very close to those averages. It does not prove that his family lived in one town for twelve centuries.

Which Road Did the Slavs Take?

Where the Slavic-speaking newcomers came from is better understood than how they reached the Balkans. A 2025 ancient-DNA study (Gretzinger et al., Nature) traced their ancestry to Eastern Europe, most probably a region spanning southern Belarus and northern Ukraine, and found it spreading west and south between the 6th and 8th centuries. In Avar-period Austria the same ancestry was already present in the 7th century (about 27% at the Mödling cemetery), mixed with local people. The study also found two shared-DNA communities of Slavic-period individuals, one north of the Carpathians and one to the south, and the authors say this may reflect two diverging waves of expansion or different degrees of mixing with local populations.

Whether the settlers of Bulgaria came through the Pannonian plain, picking up Central European ancestry on the way, or went around the Carpathians and across the lower Danube, where written sources first place the Slavs, is not settled. The two are less rivals than possible stages of one movement. No genomes survive from the lower Danube of the 6th to 8th centuries, and Minko’s DNA can show only the end result, not the road. The Avar-period comparisons above are why the question comes up: a European-type Carpathian Basin population sits as close to him as the medieval Balkans do, which fits a Pannonian pathway and fits a lower-Danube one equally well.

Sarmatians were Iron Age nomads who followed the Scythians across the Pontic steppe from about the 4th century BC and were active along the Danube and in Dacia. The Neolithic Stratum: Farmers From Anatolia

The Bronze Age herders were not the beginning. Beneath the steppe layer lies an older European foundation: the first farmers.

The mixing models give about 42% early Anatolian-farmer ancestry (about 46% on the K36-only coordinate). In similar models, modern Sardinians, who were largely bypassed by later migrations, come out at roughly 75–80% farmer ancestry. Eastern Balkan profiles sit lower because steppe ancestry arrived here in strength, including with the Slavic migrations.

The earliest European farming villages were in Greece and the southern Balkans, from about 9,000 years ago. The Balkans were the landing zone of European farming, not a way station, and Eastern Balkan genomes keep a clear trace of that first contact.

This is the ancestry behind MyHeritage’s “Greek and Albanian” and “South Italian” labels. The most direct single-letter trace is SLC24A5 (rs1426654), where Minko has two copies of the light-skin variant, a variant already common in Anatolian farmers that spread through Europe with them.

Different tests slice the same genome differently. Ancient Eurasia K6 splits it into 42.59% Near Eastern farmer-type ancestry (the component that test labels Natufian), 35.03% western hunter-gatherer-type and 19.05% ancestral North Eurasian. GEDmatch’s hunter-gatherer-versus-farmer test gives about 51% farmer (Mediterranean 31.29% plus Anatolian 20.21%), about 37% northern hunter-gatherer and 7.56% Middle Eastern herder. The slicing differs, but every test finds a large early-farmer foundation.

Takeaway Early farmers from Anatolia form the deep farming floor of this genome.

The Mesolithic Stratum: The Hunter-Gatherers

Even the first farmers were newcomers. Beneath the Neolithic layer are the hunter-gatherers who occupied Europe before agriculture.

After the last Ice Age, Europe was home to western hunter-gatherers: forest-edge and river hunters and fishers who made sophisticated stone tools. Their reference genome, Loschbour (a man who lived in what is now Luxembourg roughly 8,000 years ago), is the most distant ancient reference in every model (0.407), and the mixing models give western hunter-gatherer ancestry about 2% on its own.

That 2% is only the part the models can see separately. More hunter-gatherer ancestry travels inside the steppe share, because Yamnaya herders had already mixed with hunters on the steppe, and some inside the farmer share, because Anatolian farmers descended partly from local hunter-gatherers. The models cannot pull those apart. Calculators that pool all northern hunter-gatherer ancestry report bigger numbers (Ancient Eurasia K6 shows 35.03% western hunter-gatherer, and GEDmatch’s hunter-gatherer-versus-farmer test shows 36.92% Baltic hunter-gatherer). They measure a broader quantity than the standalone 2%, so the figures do not conflict.

The eye-color variant is the clearest hunter-gatherer trace. Minko’s genotype at rs12913832 in HERC2 is GG, the genotype most strongly associated with blue eyes in Europeans (though no single marker fully determines eye color). This variant turns up in hunter-gatherer genomes such as the La Braña man from Spain and Loschbour, thousands of years before farmers arrived.

Blue Eyes Before Fair Skin Mesolithic hunters like La Braña carried the blue-eye variant while still carrying the ancestral, darker-skin versions of the main skin-lightening genes. Even the hunters were a mix

Dig below the hunter-gatherers and there is still no single “original” people. The steppe herders were themselves a blend of two older hunter groups: hunters from the plains of Eastern Europe and hunters from the mountain valleys of the Caucasus.

In Minko’s case the Caucasus hunters sit slightly closer than the northern ones, so the profile leans a little toward the Caucasus. It is a lean, not a measurement, because early farmers from Anatolia also carried Caucasus-related ancestry, so part of that signal comes from the farmer side.

Among the Aegean and Caucasus references tested, the closest are Chalcolithic Armenia and Mycenaean Greece. That is not a recent link to Armenia or Greece. Those ancient groups were built from similar ingredients, so they sit near Minko in genetic space, like neighbors on a map, not like ancestors on a family tree.

The East Asian Check

East Asian ancestry often leaves a few sharp fingerprints in the genome. Four can be read directly from Minko’s raw file.

EDAR (rs3827760): AA. The East Asian variant (the “370A” change linked to thicker hair, more sweat glands and shovel-shaped incisors) reads as G on the forward strand this file uses, and Minko carries none. That variant is found in most East Asians and Native Americans and in almost no Europeans or Africans.

ABCC11 (rs17822931): CC. Two copies of C means wet earwax, the usual European version. The T allele, which gives dry earwax, is common in East Asia and rare in Europe.

ALDH2 (rs671): GG. The A allele, the East Asian alcohol-flush variant, is common in East Asia and nearly absent elsewhere. Minko has none.

OCA2 (rs1800414): TT. This is the ancestral version. The derived variant is common in East Asia and rare elsewhere.

What this does and does not show. A person with a small share of East Asian ancestry could still carry none of these variants, because each is inherited like a coin flip. A clean sweep rules out sizeable recent East Asian ancestry, such as a grandparent. It says little about a trace. It is a quiet check, not a proof of zero.

Why Late Antiquity Looks Far Away

In the Bulgarian ancient-DNA ladder (Layer 2), Late Antique Bulgaria sits at 0.117, while medieval Bulgaria sits at 0.034 and Early Bronze Age samples at 0.04–0.07. A genome should not be closer to people from 3,000 years earlier than to people from 1,500 years earlier unless the population in between changed. A 2023 study of Balkan skeletons found exactly that kind of change: an influx of Anatolian ancestry under Roman rule, then a large Slavic-related arrival after the 6th century, after which Balkan populations look much like those of today. Minko’s numbers fit that sequence, though a single set of distances cannot prove it.

A crossroads, not a single story

Farmers from the southeast, herders from the northeast and hunters who were here first, layered together, then mixed with the Slavs and the older peoples of the Balkans. Minko’s DNA isn’t one people. It’s the meeting place of several, which is exactly what the Eastern Balkans have always been.

The Complete Excavation: What All Seven Layers Tell Together

At the surface, the MyHeritage percentages describe the profile in modern-population terms, and those labels hide more than they show. The Global25 distances place the genome more precisely: Romanian at 0.018 and Bulgarian at 0.021, inside a tight arc of Eastern Balkan populations. The closest ancient matches, medieval Macedonia (0.030) and medieval Bulgaria (0.034), show the match is not only modern. Under that sits an older Balkan population, plus a Slavic-related layer that the 2023 Balkan ancient-DNA study puts at roughly 30–60% of present-day Balkan peoples. In Minko’s numbers it shows up as a shift away from Late Antique Bulgaria (0.117) toward the medieval population (0.034). The East Asian steppe signal of the Avar ruling elite is absent.

Deeper, the Bronze Age steppe wave and the Neolithic farmers make up almost all of the ancient structure in close to equal parts: about 52–56% steppe-related and 42–46% early farmer, with western hunter-gatherers adding about 2% on their own. The Caucasus references run slightly closer than the northern hunter-gatherer ones, and the nearest Aegean and Caucasus populations, Chalcolithic Armenia (0.100) and Mycenaean Greece (0.106), show a shared recipe of farmer plus Caucasus-related ancestry rather than recent ancestry from those places. The oldest single trait readable in the file is the HERC2 blue-eye variant that hunter-gatherers carried before farmers brought their light-skin variants.

At the base, the Neanderthal layer (about 1–2%) is shared by nearly everyone outside Africa. The single-gene checks agree with the European picture throughout, and the East Asian markers (EDAR, ABCC11, ALDH2, OCA2) all show the ancestral version. The different methods used here all put the genome in the same place: the Eastern Balkan crossroads.

In Plain Terms: The Whole Excavation, Start to Finish

Run the story from the top down and it reads like peeling paint off an old house. Each coat is newer than the one beneath it, and none has erased the others. At the surface is a man whose closest living relatives are Romanians and Bulgarians. Peel back a few centuries and there are the early medieval Balkans, where Slavic-speaking newcomers mixed with the people already living there. Peel back further and those people appear: Thracians and their neighbors, who farmed and herded this land long before any Slavic speaker arrived.

Behind them is a story of whole populations on the move. About five thousand years ago, herders from the steppe north of the Black Sea swept west and reshaped Europe’s DNA, meeting the descendants of farmers who had walked out of Anatolia three thousand years or so earlier. Before the farmers, the land belonged to hunter-gatherers who never farmed, people whose blue-eye variant still shows up in Minko’s DNA. Beneath even that sits a faint trace of Neanderthal ancestry, shared with nearly everyone outside Africa.

Takeaway: One continuous story: hunter-gatherers → farmers → steppe herders → Thracians and neighbors → Slavic-era mixing → the modern Eastern Balkans. Neanderthal DNA is a small, shared archaic background, not a separate branch.
