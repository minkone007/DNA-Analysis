Eight Calculators, One Genome — Reading Past the Labels

Run the same raw DNA through eight different admixture calculators and you get eight different vocabularies for the same underlying genome. MDLP K23b reads this profile as 37.54% Caucasian. Dodecad K7b reads the same genome as 52.46% Atlantic_Baltic. HarappaWorld calls its largest component 31.91% NE-Euro. These are not three contradictory findings — they are three reference panels drawing different boundaries around the same genetic variation, each calculator's "Caucasian," "Atlantic_Baltic," and "NE-Euro" labels overlapping heavily in the populations they actually sample from.

What is consistent across all eight calculators is the underlying structure, not the labels: a large Caucasus/West Asian-leaning component (MDLP K23b Caucasian 37.54%, MDLP K16 Caucasian 33.72%, Dodecad K12b Caucasus 27.75%, HarappaWorld Caucasian 26.24%), a large North European/steppe-descended component (Dodecad K7b Atlantic_Baltic 52.46%, Eurogenes K13 Baltic 22.85%, HarappaWorld NE-Euro 31.91%), and a consistent Mediterranean/farmer-descended component (Eurogenes K13 East Med + West Med ≈ 41%, Dodecad K12b Atlantic_Med 26.57%, HarappaWorld Mediterranean 24.22%). Three streams, eight vocabularies.

The Oracle single-population approximations cut through the labelling entirely by asking a more direct question: which real, named populations does this genome most resemble? Across every calculator that offers this feature, the answer converges tightly. Eurogenes K13: Bulgarian 5.22% distance, Romanian 8.09%. EUtest K15: Bulgarian 5.11%, Romanian 8.13%. HarappaWorld: Romanian 4.96%, Bulgarian 5.20%. Three independent calculators, three independent reference panels, the same two populations occupying the top two positions every time. That convergence — not any single percentage — is the reliable signal in this dataset.

The Living Population Map — A Slightly Different Centre of Gravity

The Global25 modern-population table places the closest match at Gagauz, distance 0.0161 — narrowly ahead of Bulgarian at 0.0172 and Romanian at 0.0197. The Gagauz are a Turkic-speaking Orthodox Christian minority concentrated in southern Moldova and Bessarabian Ukraine; despite the Turkic language, ancient and modern DNA studies consistently show the Gagauz are genetically indistinguishable from their Bulgarian and Romanian neighbours — a population that underwent language replacement without a corresponding genetic replacement. A top match to Gagauz is therefore not a signal of Turkic ancestry; it is the algorithm confirming, from a slightly different angle, the same Eastern Balkan / Lower Danube position that Bulgarian and Romanian already indicate.

Macedonian (0.0242), Greek Central Macedonia (0.0277), Montenegrin (0.0291), Albanian and Serbian (both 0.0311), and Moldovan (0.0332) round out the top nine, all still within a tight, coherent Balkan cluster. What stands out against Minko's own top-nine table is the position of Greek Central Macedonia: it ranks 5th here at 0.0277, compared to 8th at 0.040 in Minko's table — a meaningfully closer Aegean-facing pull, while Albanian sits essentially level with Serbian rather than trailing it. Read alongside the K36 breakdown below, this is consistent with — though not proof of — a somewhat more southerly, Aegean-oriented weighting in this profile relative to Minko's, alongside the same core Eastern Balkan identity both brothers share.

The Medieval Stratum — Closer to Home Than Minko's Profile, Identical Beyond It

Model 3 confirms the single most striking number in this whole dataset: Bulgaria Medieval at distance 0.0267 — noticeably closer than the equivalent figure in Minko's report (0.034). This is a distance to actual ancient DNA recovered from individuals buried in Bulgaria during the First and Second Bulgarian Empire period, not a modelled or theoretical reference. A tighter distance here means this genome's coordinate sits nearer the centre of the medieval Bulgarian genetic variation than Minko's does.

What's genuinely striking is what happens beyond Bulgaria Medieval itself: the rest of the medieval chain is nearly identical between the two brothers. Hungary Late Avar sits at 0.1225 here versus 0.122 for Minko. Serbia Medieval Gepidian: 0.1382 versus 0.136. Hungary Early Avar: 0.2152 versus 0.214. Hungary Late Avar Elite: 0.3029 versus 0.301. Hungary Early Avar Elite: 0.4162 versus 0.414. Every one of these reference points — none of them Bulgarian, all of them describing the broader Pannonian and Viking-era context the Slavic migration passed through — lands within a few thousandths of Minko's figure. That pattern points to something specific: the brothers' shared inheritance of the wider medieval Balkan/Pannonian genetic context is essentially fixed, while the one number that actually differs between them — the direct Bulgaria Medieval distance — is exactly the number carrying the sibling-specific signal.

Bulgaria Medieval oEastAsia, a medieval Bulgarian outlier individual carrying an additional East Asian-related admixture signal (plausibly linked to Bulgar, Pecheneg, or Cuman contact), sits at 0.0685 — far enough from the general medieval population (0.0267) to serve as a useful negative control: this genome does not carry an elevated version of that specific East Asian-admixed medieval channel.

The Neolithic Stratum — Danube Farming Villages

Two named Bulgarian Neolithic sites appear directly in the stratigraphy panel: Malak Preslavets (distance 0.0934, with a lower-Early-European-Farmer outlier variant at 0.0994) and Krepost (0.1164). Both are real excavated Early Neolithic farming settlements in the Danube basin, among the earliest agricultural communities in the Balkans, dated to roughly the 6th millennium BC — the same landfall generation of Anatolian farmers discussed in Minko's report and confirmed here by the Boncuklu/Barcin signal in Layer 3. Their presence this far down the distance ranking, well past the Bronze Age and Medieval clusters, is exactly what's expected: they represent the population before the steppe migrations reshaped the region, not after.

The "oLowEEF" tag on the Malak Preslavets outlier individual denotes a burial with a lower-than-typical proportion of Early European Farmer ancestry for that site — a reminder that even a single Neolithic village was not genetically uniform. The general Bulgaria N reference (0.1524) and its outlier variant, Bulgaria N_o (0.1247), sit at the far end of the entire panel — the oldest and most distant tier, exactly as in Minko's profile, confirming the deepest shared floor between the brothers and Bulgaria's ancient population is this same Early Neolithic farming stratum.

The Mesolithic Stratum — Western Hunter-Gatherers, Present but Diluted

Luxembourg Loschbour, the Western Hunter-Gatherer reference, is the most distant population in every model that includes it — 0.4133 in both the three-wave model (Layer 3) and the deep archaic model (Layer 6) — confirming that WHG ancestry, while real, is the smallest of the three founding European streams in this genome, in the same relative position it occupies in Minko's profile. Because WHG ancestry arrives partly as a standalone Balkan-local hunter-gatherer contribution and partly hidden inside the Yamnaya steppe component itself (the Yamnaya's own ancestors already carried WHG-related ancestry from earlier mixing on the European steppe), a single distance figure like this one cannot cleanly separate those two pathways — that would require the same kind of qualified, multi-source Stone Age decomposition used in Minko's Ancient Eurasia K6 breakdown, which hasn't been run for this kit yet.

A Note on the Heat Map The shared-segment (IBD) chromosome-painting heat map submitted alongside these models is a different kind of evidence from the G25 distance tables above — it shows actual matching DNA segments against named ancient individuals rather than modelled coordinate distances. Several rows in that map are genuine sequenced Neanderthal genomes (El Sidron, Spain; three Vindija Cave individuals from Croatia; Mezmaiskaya, Russia), alongside Denisovan and Upper Palaeolithic Siberian references (Denisova, Altai, Kostenki14, Ust'-Ishim). Segment matches in the 0.5–2 cM range against archaic individuals are expected background — everyone outside Africa carries measurable Neanderthal-derived DNA at this scale — and are consistent with, though not a substitute for, the single-SNP Neanderthal genotype (rs12041331 GG) documented in Minko's report. A proper write-up of this heat map — which chromosomes, which specific segment lengths, against which of the Neanderthal rows specifically — would need the underlying segment table (chromosome, start/end position, cM length) rather than the rendered image alone.

Reading the Two Brothers Side by Side

With all five Vahaduo models now in hand, the picture is no longer just suggestive — it's a consistent, multiply-replicated pattern across every independent model, without either sibling stepping outside the same core Eastern Balkan identity:

1. A different closest modern match, same neighbourhood

Minko's closest modern populations are Romanian (0.018) then Bulgarian (0.021). Here the order is Gagauz (0.0161), Bulgarian (0.0172), then Romanian (0.0197). Gagauz is genetically a Bulgarian/Romanian-adjacent population under a Turkic language label, so this is not a different genetic centre of gravity — it's the same Lower Danube / Eastern Balkan cluster, approached from a very slightly different point within it.

2. The medieval "background" is nearly identical — only Bulgaria Medieval itself differs

This is the most precise finding in the whole comparison. Every non-Bulgarian medieval reference — Hungary Late Avar, Serbia Medieval Gepidian, Hungary Early Avar, both Avar Elite tiers — lands within thousandths of Minko's corresponding figure (see Layer 2 table). Only Bulgaria Medieval itself differs meaningfully: 0.0267 here versus 0.034 for Minko. That contrast is informative in its own right — it suggests the brothers' shared inheritance of the broader medieval Balkan/Pannonian context is essentially fixed, and the one place their genomes genuinely diverge is in exactly how close each sits to the core Bulgarian medieval population specifically.

3. A farmer-leaning Bronze Age balance where Minko's was a tie

Minko: Yamnaya (0.171) ≈ Barcin (0.173) — a gap of 0.002, essentially balanced. Stayko: Barcin (0.1613) clearly closer than Yamnaya (0.1841) — a gap of 0.023, more than ten times larger. This is a genuine structural difference in the steppe-versus-farmer balance between the two brothers' Bronze Age signal, not a rounding artefact.

4. An across-the-board Aegean/South-Caucasus shift — eight populations, eight confirmations

The Model 5 Caucasus-resolution table is the clearest evidence in the entire report: every one of the eight shared populations between Minko's and Stayko's Model 5 runs is closer for Stayko — Armenia C Areni, Greece BA Mycenaean, Greece Minoan Lassithi, Armenia EBA Kura-Araxes Berkaber, Turkey Barcin LN, Iran C SehGabi, Georgia Kotias CHG, and Iran GanjDareh N (see Layer 6 table for exact figures). Eight independent populations, eight consistent results, none of them marginal. Combined with the Bronze Age farmer-lean (point 3) and Greek Central Macedonia's tighter modern-population rank (5th here versus 8th for Minko), this is no longer just a suggestive pattern — it is a well-corroborated, multiply-independent finding that this genome sits measurably closer to the Aegean/South Caucasus Bronze Age corridor than Minko's does, while both remain solidly within the same Eastern Balkan crossroads population.

5. The CHG-over-EHG pattern repeats independently, with a minor twist

Both brothers show Georgia Kotias (CHG) closer than Russia Karelia (EHG) in the deep ancestral model — 0.2508 vs 0.2956 here, 0.253 vs 0.286 for Minko — a shared, inherited structural feature rather than something either genome would show by chance. One small reordering: GanjDareh N (0.2910) edges narrowly ahead of Karelia (0.2956) here, whereas Karelia (0.285) was closer than GanjDareh (0.292) in Minko's data. The gap involved is under 0.01 in both cases — plausibly noise, flagged rather than glossed over.

What Would Settle the Aegean-Lean With Real Numbers Five independent Vahaduo models now agree on direction: this genome sits closer to the Aegean/South-Caucasus Bronze Age world and to Bulgaria's medieval population specifically, while sharing an essentially identical broader medieval/Pannonian background with Minko. That's a strong, well-triangulated qualitative picture. The one step that would turn "closer across the board" into an actual measured distance is the vector comparison between Minko's and Stayko's consensus G25 coordinates directly — subtracting one 25-dimension vector from the other rather than comparing each separately to a set of ancient populations. Stayko's coordinate row is now on hand; once Minko's own consensus coordinate row is available, that comparison can be computed directly.

The Complete Excavation — What All Seven Layers Tell Together

With all five Vahaduo models now run, the excavation is structurally complete: this genome places in the same Eastern Balkan crossroads position documented in Minko's report — Anatolian Neolithic farmers, Pontic steppe pastoralists, and European hunter-gatherers converging over roughly 8,000 years — with a consistent, multiply-replicated lean further south and east toward the Aegean and South Caucasus than his profile shows.

At the surface, the modern-population table places the closest matches at Gagauz (0.0161), Bulgarian (0.0172), and Romanian (0.0197) — the same Lower Danube cluster as Minko's, approached from a slightly different point within it. The medieval layer reveals something precise: the broader medieval/Pannonian background (Avar, Viking, Gepidian references) is essentially identical between the brothers, while the direct Bulgaria Medieval distance is not — 0.0267 here versus 0.034 for Minko, the sharpest single point of genuine divergence in the whole comparison.

The Bronze Age layer sharpens this further: where Minko's genome sat almost perfectly balanced between the Yamnaya steppe signal and the Anatolian Barcin farmer signal (a gap of just 0.002), this genome leans measurably toward the farmer signal (a gap of 0.023) — a real structural asymmetry, not noise. The Neolithic tier — Malak Preslavets (0.0934) and Krepost (0.1164) — remains, as in Minko's profile, the most distant tier in the Bulgarian panel, the shared floor beneath both brothers' more recent signal.

The deep archaic model reproduces the same CHG-over-EHG weighting found in Minko's genome (Kotias 0.2508 versus Karelia 0.2956) — a family-level structural feature, not a coincidence specific to one sibling — while the purpose-built Model 5 Caucasus-resolution table delivers the clearest evidence of all: all eight shared populations in that model are closer to this genome than to Minko's, with no exceptions. Taken together — the farmer-leaning Bronze Age balance, the tighter Bulgaria Medieval distance, Greek Central Macedonia's higher modern-population rank, and eight-for-eight closer Aegean/Caucasus distances — this is a well-triangulated, multiply-independent finding rather than a single suggestive number.

What remains genuinely open is not direction but magnitude: exactly how large the displacement between the two brothers' ancestry actually is, measured directly rather than inferred from parallel distance tables. That answer waits on Minko's own consensus G25 coordinate row, which would let the comparison be computed as an actual 25-dimension vector difference rather than pieced together from populations both genomes were separately compared against. The SNP genotype table, MyHeritage percentages, and chromosome-level detail on the heat map's Neanderthal segment matches remain the other open items for bringing this report to full parity with Minko's.
