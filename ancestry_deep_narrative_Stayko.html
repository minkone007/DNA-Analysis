What the Percentages Are Telling You — and What They Are Not

MyHeritage's ethnicity estimate for this kit reads as follows:

Greek and Albanian (North Macedonia and Greece)43.1%
Balkan41.7%
East European9.5%
South Italian5.7%

MyHeritage also flags four Additional Genetic Groups detected at lower confidence — finer-grained population signals the main breakdown doesn't have room for: Bulgaria, Serbia/Croatia/Bosnia and Herzegovina, Romania, and, unexpectedly, Ireland (Kerry, Cork and Limerick).

Read literally, these four headline percentages look like a genealogical pie chart — as if 43.1% of this genome's family tree runs through Greece and Albania, 5.7% through southern Italy. That is not what the numbers mean. MyHeritage's algorithm is comparing this genome's genetic variation against reference panels built from present-day people who identify with each labelled region, and assigning the closest-fitting mixture of those panels. "Greek and Albanian" and "Balkan" are not two separate ancestral populations that mixed at some point — they are two overlapping, imperfectly-drawn boxes around the same broad Southeast European Paleo-Balkan gene pool, split apart largely because MyHeritage's reference panel happens to distinguish northern Greece/Albania/North Macedonia from the wider Balkan interior.

Greek and Albanian (43.1%) does not mean a Greek or Albanian grandparent. It means this genome's genetic profile most closely resembles those modern reference populations — themselves descendants of the same ancient Aegean, Anatolian Neolithic, and Paleo-Balkan substrate that underlies all of southeastern Europe, the same substrate that also produced the ancient Thracians, Dacians, and Illyrians of the pre-Slavic Balkans. Modern Greeks and Albanians carry a high proportion of Early European Farmer ancestry and comparatively less of the later Slavic migration signal, making them the closest living proxies for that pre-Slavic Paleo-Balkan layer. Balkan (41.7%) captures the other half of the story — the Slavic migration-era ancestry and the local population already living in the Balkans before the 6th-century Slavic expansions arrived to join them. Together, at 43.1% + 41.7% = 84.8% combined, this genome sits almost exactly at the midpoint between the pre-Slavic Paleo-Balkan substrate and the Slavic migration-era layer — the same crossroads position that defines the Eastern Balkans as a genetic region.

East European (9.5%)v most likely reflects the northward edge of that same continuum — the genetic gradient running up through Romania, Moldova, and Ukraine that any Balkan population shares to some degree with its northern neighbours, and very plausibly a share of the same northern Slavic-homeland signal (the Baltic and forest-zone ancestry carried down from the Slavic migration's origin near the Pripyat marshes) discussed further in Layer 2. South Italian (5.7%) is very likely doing similar work to what it does in comparable Balkan profiles elsewhere: southern Italy and the Balkans share a deep layer of common Neolithic and Bronze Age Mediterranean ancestry, and reference panels built from modern Italians and modern Balkan populations partially overlap as a result — a shared ancient substrate showing up as a modern-nation label, not evidence of a genealogical Italian ancestor.

The Additional Genetic Groups are the more interesting layer here, because three of the four — Bulgaria, Serbia/Croatia/Bosnia and Herzegovina, Romania — simply confirm, at finer resolution, exactly the Eastern Balkan / Lower Danube position that the G25 modern-population table in Layer 1 independently arrives at through a completely different method. The fourth, Ireland, does not fit that pattern at all, and is addressed directly in Notable Findings below rather than folded quietly into the main narrative.

Independent admixture calculators built on entirely different reference panels agree on the underlying structure even where their labels diverge: MDLP K23b reads this profile as 37.54% Caucasian; Dodecad K7b reads the same genome as 52.46% Atlantic_Baltic; HarappaWorld calls its largest component 31.91% NE-Euro. Three calculators, three different largest-component labels — because each is drawing its category boundaries in a different place across the same underlying variation: a Caucasus/West Asian-leaning layer (Kotias-related), a North European/steppe-descended layer (Yamnaya-related), and a Mediterranean/farmer-descended layer (Anatolian-related). The Oracle single-population approximations available in several calculators cut through the labelling by asking a more direct question — which real, named populations does this genome most resemble — and converge tightly: Eurogenes K13 places Bulgarian at 5.22% distance and Romanian at 8.09%; EUtest K15 places Bulgarian at 5.11% and Romanian at 8.13%; HarappaWorld places Romanian at 4.96% and Bulgarian at 5.20%. Three independent panels, the same two populations occupying the top two positions every time.

In Plain Terms This layer is really about one idea: the label is not the finding. A percentage next to a country name looks precise, but it's really the output of a computer program comparing this genome to a reference panel someone else built, using categories that program's designers happened to choose. Change the program, and the label changes too — "Caucasian" in one becomes "NE-Euro" in another, even though they're describing overlapping stretches of the same underlying DNA. What you can trust is agreement across several differently-built tools: when Eurogenes, EUtest, and HarappaWorld — three calculators built by different people, using different reference populations — all put Bulgarian and Romanian at the very top of their lists, that convergence is real evidence. A single calculator's exact percentage is a rough sketch; several calculators landing on the same two answers is the actual signal worth trusting.

The Living Population Map — Eastern Balkans, With a Pull Toward the Aegean

The Global25 modern-population table places the closest match at Gagauz, distance 0.0161 — narrowly ahead of Bulgarian at 0.0172 and Romanian at 0.0197. The Gagauz are a Turkic-speaking Orthodox Christian minority concentrated in southern Moldova and Bessarabian Ukraine; despite the Turkic language, ancient and modern DNA studies consistently show the Gagauz are genetically indistinguishable from their Bulgarian and Romanian neighbours — a population that underwent language replacement without a corresponding genetic replacement. A top match to Gagauz is therefore not a signal of Turkic ancestry; it is the algorithm confirming, from a slightly different angle, the same Eastern Balkan / Lower Danube position that Bulgarian and Romanian already indicate — and that MyHeritage's Additional Genetic Groups (Bulgaria, Serbia/Croatia/Bosnia, Romania) already pointed to in Layer 0.

Macedonian (0.0242), Greek Central Macedonia (0.0277), Montenegrin (0.0291), Albanian and Serbian (both 0.0311), and Moldovan (0.0332) round out the top nine, all within a tight, coherent Balkan cluster. Greek Central Macedonia's position — 5th, closer than Montenegrin, Albanian, and Serbian — is worth noting on its own terms: it puts a specifically Aegean-facing Greek population inside the tightest cluster of matches rather than trailing behind it, consistent with the MyHeritage "Greek and Albanian" component carrying real weight rather than being an artefact of overlapping reference panels.

A distance this small means this genome's G25 coordinate sits very near the centre of the medieval Bulgarian genetic variation itself.

The rest of the medieval panel places that number in context. The Slavic Migration — Pannonian Routing

The Avar figures in the table above aren't just background noise — read in sequence, they trace a specific historical process. The Avar Khaganate controlled the Pannonian Basin (modern Hungary) from 567 to 895 AD, precisely spanning the period of the Slavic migrations into the Balkans. Early Avars (6th–7th century) carried substantial East Asian steppe ancestry and sit far from this genome at 0.2152; Late Avars (7th–9th century) were already heavily admixed with local European populations by the time this distance was measured, and sit far closer at 0.1225. The elite Avar burials sharpen the picture further: Hungary Late Avar Elite (0.3029) and Hungary Early Avar Elite (0.4162) — both associated with the steppe-descended ruling dynasty rather than the general population — sit dramatically further away than either general Avar tier. What's genetically close to this profile isn't the East Asian ruling class of the Avar Khaganate; it's the already-Europeanised general population being absorbed into it, which is exactly the population the Slavic migrations moved through and merged with on their way south into the Balkans.

The Slavic Migration — Pannonian Routing A narrative historical model consistent with these genetic coordinates suggests a substantial Slavic migration-era contribution, layered on top of a larger pre-Slavic Paleo-Balkan substrate — though these are directional readings of the data, not formally validated ancestry proportions. The oracle 4-population combinations available in this dataset's admixture calculators consistently surface Croatian, Moldavian, Serbian, and Bulgarian proxies alongside Sardinian (Mediterranean farmer signal) and Armenian/Georgian Jewish/Turkish references (Anatolian-Caucasus signal) — a mix of Central-European-routed Slavic populations rather than direct Russian or Ukrainian ones, the signature of a Slavic population that had already absorbed Central European elements before reaching the Balkans. The route this data points to is the Pannonian corridor: down through the Carpathian basin, through the territory the Avar Khaganate controlled, into the Balkans — not a direct line east from the Slavic homeland.

The extended Bulgarian stratigraphic panel — 25 populations spanning Neolithic to Late Antiquity — shows a mostly coherent chronological gradient, closest to Medieval and the Bronze Age cluster below, more distant moving toward the Neolithic. One irregularity worth flagging honestly: Bulgaria Late Antiquity (0.1064) sits further out than the Iron Age, Late Iron Age, and Early Iron Age samples that immediately precede it chronologically (0.0774–0.0854) — likely a small-sample or reference-composition effect rather than a genuine reversal, worth treating as noise unless it recurs in an independent re-run.

The distance this genome travels across that table, from the Neolithic tier down to the Medieval tier at the top, is the genetic record of the same historical layering — Thracian-descended Paleo-Balkan substrate first, Bronze Age steppe admixture next, Slavic migration-era synthesis last.
In Plain Terms The number to hold onto from this whole layer is 0.0267 — the distance to real, excavated skeletons from medieval Bulgaria. Everything else in this layer is there to give that number context, in two directions. First, sideways: the Avar numbers tell a specific historical story — this genome sits close to the ordinary population that got absorbed into the Avar-controlled Pannonian corridor as Slavic migrants moved south into the Balkans, and far from the steppe-descended ruling class that governed over them. In Plain Terms Picture two rivers merging into one — one flowing up from the old farming villages of Anatolia, the other down from the horse-herding steppe north of the Black Sea — and this genome sits a little further along the Anatolian bank than the steppe one. That's a real, measurable lean, not a coin-flip either way — and it's the same lean toward the older, more southern population that keeps showing up across other parts of this report, from the modern-population rankings to the admixture calculators. Small signals pointing the same direction, over and over, add up to something worth taking seriously.

The Neolithic Stratum — Danube Farming Villages

Two named Bulgarian Neolithic sites appear directly in the stratigraphy panel: Malak Preslavets (distance 0.0934, with a lower-Early-European-Farmer outlier variant at 0.0994) and Krepost (0.1164). Both are real excavated Early Neolithic farming settlements in the Danube basin, among the earliest agricultural communities in the Balkans, dated to roughly the 6th millennium BC — the same landfall generation of Anatolian farmers confirmed by the Boncuklu/Barcin signal in Layer 3. Their presence this far down the distance ranking, well past the Bronze Age and Medieval clusters, is exactly what's expected: they represent the population before the steppe migrations reshaped the region, not after.

The "oLowEEF" tag on the Malak Preslavets outlier individual denotes a burial with a lower-than-typical proportion of Early European Farmer ancestry for that site — a reminder that even a single Neolithic village was not genetically uniform. The general Bulgaria N reference (0.1524) and its outlier variant, Bulgaria N_o (0.1247), sit at the far end of the entire panel — the oldest and most distant tier, confirming the deepest floor this genome and Bulgaria's ancient population still share is this same Early Neolithic farming stratum.

In Plain Terms This is the oldest solid ground in the whole excavation. Go back far enough and every line of evidence in this report — the modern populations, the medieval graves, the Bronze Age cluster — eventually bottoms out at the same place: small farming villages on the Danube, around eight thousand years old, from back when farming itself was still a new idea in Europe. Everything built on top of that — the steppe migrations, the medieval kingdoms, the modern nations — is a later addition to a foundation that was laid here first.

The Mesolithic Stratum — Western Hunter-Gatherers, Present but Diluted

Luxembourg Loschbour, the Western Hunter-Gatherer reference, is the most distant population in every model that includes it — 0.4133 in both the three-wave model (Layer 3) and the deep archaic model (Layer 6) — confirming that WHG ancestry, while real, is the smallest of the three founding European streams present in this genome. Because WHG ancestry arrives partly as a standalone Balkan-local hunter-gatherer contribution and partly hidden inside the Yamnaya steppe component itself (the Yamnaya's own ancestors already carried WHG-related ancestry from earlier mixing on the European steppe), a single distance figure like this one cannot cleanly separate those two pathways — that would require a qualified, multi-source Stone Age decomposition model, which hasn't been run for this kit yet.

The shared-segment (IBD) heat map submitted alongside the G25 models adds a second, independent line of evidence here: this genome carries two identifiable matching segments against the sequenced Loschbour genome itself. Loschbour is one of the best-characterised Western Hunter-Gatherer references in ancient DNA — an 8,000-year-old individual from Luxembourg sequenced to high coverage — which makes a direct segment match to it more informative than a segment match to a lower-quality or less-studied ancient sample would be. The correct reading of this is specific: this genome shares a detectable stretch of DNA with the Loschbour individual himself, not "this genome is X% WHG." The G25 distance above is what actually places WHG ancestry in proportion relative to the other founding streams; the segment match is a direct, physical confirmation that a real WHG-related genetic connection exists, sitting alongside that proportional estimate rather than replacing it.

In Plain Terms Two different kinds of evidence, two different questions, and it matters not to mix them up. The distance number answers "how much, relative to everything else?" — and the answer is: WHG is real but the smallest of the three main ingredients. The segment match answers a completely different question: "is there an actual, physical stretch of shared DNA with this specific ancient person?" — and here, yes, there genuinely is, twice over. Think of it like a family recipe: the distance number tells you WHG is a minor ingredient rather than a major one, while the segment match is like finding an actual heirloom spice jar in the cupboard that once belonged to that ingredient's original source. Useful, concrete evidence — but it can't tell you the recipe's overall proportions on its own.

A Note on the Heat Map The shared-segment (IBD) heat map submitted alongside these models includes five sequenced Neanderthal genomes — El Sidron (Spain), three Vindija Cave individuals (Croatia), and Mezmaiskaya (Russia) — and none of the five show a matching segment for this genome at the standard detection threshold. The same absence held even when the comparison was re-run at much more permissive thresholds, down to a level so lenient that clearly unrelated ancient individuals (including a 12,500-year-old Native American reference) began showing near-continuous matches purely from background genetic noise. The Neanderthal rows never joined that noise floor — a small number of isolated single-segment hits at best, sharply different from the saturation seen elsewhere. That contrast is itself informative: it confirms segment-matching against one individual archaic genome simply isn't the right tool for detecting Neanderthal ancestry, which is why it's measured genome-wide via SNP-based methods instead, not through tools like this one. The heat map's genuinely informative results remain the two confirmed hits described above — Loschbour (WHG) and Kotias (CHG).

First, the actual finding: this genome sits closer to the Bronze Age Aegean and South Caucasus world — Mycenaean Greece, Armenia's Kura-Araxes culture — than to almost anything else tested in this entire report, including the Anatolian farming reference from earlier layers. That's a real, specific, and quite striking result. Second, a myth worth quietly retiring: matching a segment of DNA to one particular 40,000-year-old Neanderthal skeleton was never going to be the way to detect Neanderthal ancestry — it's a bit like trying to prove you have Irish ancestry by checking whether you share a specific street address with one particular Irish person from centuries ago. The right tool for that job compares your entire genome's DNA letters against thousands of Neanderthal positions at once, not this kind of segment-by-segment search. Its absence here says nothing at all about whether typical background Neanderthal ancestry is present — it almost certainly is, the same as it is for virtually everyone with non-African ancestry.

EDAR and ABCC11 — A Genuinely Interesting Combination This is the one finding in the table above worth pausing on. EDAR-370A (the East Asian-associated hair/sweat-gland variant) and the ABCC11 dry-earwax allele are both products of the same broad East Asian selective sweep, and in East Asian genomes today they very strongly co-occur — carrying one usually means carrying the other. This genome carries EDAR-370A in homozygous form (A;A) without the linked ABCC11 derived allele (it's ancestral C;C instead). That decoupling is genuinely interesting: it's more consistent with the EDAR variant having entered this lineage through an older, indirect channel — some ancient Bronze Age Eurasian steppe populations carried low frequencies of EDAR-370A well before, and independently of, the specific East Asian sweep that later locked EDAR and ABCC11 together — than with a direct, recent East Asian ancestor (say, from medieval Turkic-era contact), which would be expected to bring the linked ABCC11 allele along with it. This is a hypothesis worth holding loosely, not a settled conclusion — but it's a real, specific, checkable pattern rather than a vague impression.
In Plain Terms Most of the markers in the table above are simple, settled facts: brown eyes rather than blue, light skin, unable to comfortably digest milk as an adult — the same everyday genetic traits reported on personal DNA tests everywhere, just with the population-history context added in. The EDAR/ABCC11 pair is the one genuine puzzle. In East Asian populations, these two genes almost always travel together, like two siblings who are rarely seen apart — they were pulled along by the same wave of natural selection thousands of years ago. Here, one sibling showed up without the other, which is a specific, unusual pattern. The most reasonable explanation isn't a hidden recent East Asian ancestor; it's an older, more roundabout journey — this particular gene variant possibly hitching a ride with steppe populations moving across Eurasia in the Bronze Age, long before the East Asian selective sweep that later bundled the two genes together. It's a genuinely interesting thread, but it should be held as an open question, not a solved case.

Notable Findings

Four signals across this dataset stand out from the rest — either because they cut against the main narrative, because they resolve something the main layers left open, or because they sharpen into focus once set beside a close relative's profile.

1. The Ireland Signal — Now With Segment-Level Support

Of the four MyHeritage Additional Genetic Groups, three — Bulgaria, Serbia/Croatia/Bosnia and Herzegovina, Romania — simply confirm the Eastern Balkan position at finer resolution. The fourth, Ireland (Kerry, Cork and Limerick), looked at first like it might be an artefact of overlapping reference panels rather than a real signal — Additional Genetic Groups are lower-confidence by design, and nothing in the G25 modelling independently pointed toward Ireland. The IBD segment heat map changes that assessment: this genome carries identifiable matching segments against two named ancient Irish individuals — two segments against Rathlin1 (Bronze Age Ireland, ~2000 BC) and two segments against Ballynahatty (Neolithic Ireland, ~3200 BC). That's a genuine, independent line of evidence the MyHeritage estimate alone didn't have.

The most reasonable reading still isn't "recent Irish ancestry." Neolithic and Bronze Age Ireland and the Neolithic/Bronze Age Balkans both drew on the same deep ancestral currents — Anatolian-derived farmers moving out across Europe, followed by steppe pastoralist expansion reaching most of the continent including the British Isles — so a segment shared with an ancient Irish individual most plausibly reflects that common deep substrate rather than a direct genealogical line to Ireland specifically. But it's a more concrete finding than before: a real, located, and now twice-corroborated segment match, not just a faint statistical echo in a lower-confidence ethnicity label.

2. A CHG-Weighted Steppe Signal

Georgia Kotias (Caucasus Hunter-Gatherer) ranks closer to this genome (0.2508) than Russia Karelia (Eastern Hunter-Gatherer, 0.2956) in the deep-ancestry model. Since the Yamnaya steppe pastoralists were themselves a roughly even merger of CHG and EHG ancestry, a genome weighted this way toward the CHG parent carries the specific steppe signature characteristic of southeastern European Yamnaya-descended populations — distinguishable, at least in principle, from the more EHG-heavy weighting typical of the northern Corded Ware-descended populations of Central and Northern Europe.

3. A Farmer-Weighted Bronze Age Balance

The clearest quantitative asymmetry in the whole dataset: Turkey Barcin LN (Anatolian farmer, 0.1613) sits noticeably closer than Russia Samara EBA Yamnaya (steppe pastoralist, 0.1841) in the three-wave model — a gap of 0.023, reinforced independently by Model 5, where Armenian Chalcolithic Areni (0.0921) and Bronze Age Mycenaean Greece (0.0938) rank closer than any other ancient population tested, including the Anatolian farmer reference itself. Three independent models point the same direction: whatever combination of Anatolian farmer and steppe pastoralist ancestry assembled this genome's Bronze Age layer, the farmer/Aegean side of that mixture carries somewhat more weight than the steppe side.

4. An EDAR Variant Without Its Usual Partner

Homozygous EDAR-370A (A;A) without the linked ABCC11 dry-earwax allele (this genome carries the ancestral C;C instead) is a genuinely specific, checkable pattern — see the Genetic Markers section above for the full reasoning. In brief: because these two variants are normally inherited together as part of the same East Asian selective sweep, carrying one without the other is more consistent with an old, indirect route into this lineage — plausibly Bronze Age steppe-mediated gene flow — than with recent, direct East Asian ancestry.

5. A Note on Family Context

Stayko and his brother Minko Nenov were analysed using the same overall framework — MyHeritage-derived ancestry information, Global25 modelling, ancient-population distance panels, Bulgarian archaeological references, admixture calculators, and selected ancestry-informative SNPs. The comparison is useful precisely because the two genomes share the overwhelming majority of their recent ancestry while showing measurable differences in how that shared ancestry is distributed across the genetic reference space.

Stayko's profile is particularly notable for a somewhat stronger southern/Aegean–Mediterranean and Caucasus-facing signal. His modern-population matches remain firmly within the Balkan continuum, but his position within that continuum is shifted somewhat toward the southern end: Gagauz 0.0161, Bulgarian 0.0172, Romanian 0.0197, Macedonian 0.0243, and Greek Central Macedonia 0.0277. Stayko occupies a tighter southeastern/Eastern-Balkan position with a stronger Aegean–Caucasus-facing pull, whereas Minko sits somewhat farther from the southern Balkan references and relatively closer to the broader northern Balkan/Romanian–Bulgarian cluster.

The Full
Picture

The Complete Excavation — What All Seven Layers Tell Together

Seven layers of increasing depth, and five independent statistical models, converge on a single coherent story: this genome sits at the crossroads of three founding ancestral streams that assembled the population of the Eastern Balkans over roughly 8,000 years — Anatolian Neolithic farmers, Pontic steppe pastoralists, and the deeper Caucasus and Iranian populations that fed into the steppe wave before it ever crossed into Europe — with a distinctive lean toward the Aegean and South Caucasus corner of that convergence.

At the surface, MyHeritage's Greek and Albanian (43.1%) and Balkan (41.7%) components, combined at nearly 85%, describe one thing under two overlapping labels: a genome sitting almost entirely within the Southeast European genetic continuum. The G25 modern-population table confirms this independently and at finer resolution — Gagauz, Bulgarian, and Romanian occupying the top three positions, with Greek Central Macedonia close behind in 5th place, ahead of Montenegrin, Albanian, and Serbian.

Moving deeper, the medieval layer anchors this genome unusually close to actual ancient DNA recovered from Bulgaria's First and Second Empire period (distance 0.0267), embedded within a coherent, steadily widening ring of medieval Balkan and Pannonian reference points — Viking-era Rus' contact, then the Avar Khaganate, then its more distant nomadic elite. The Bronze Age layer resolves a genuine asymmetry: the Anatolian farmer signal (Barcin, 0.1613) outweighs the steppe pastoralist signal (Yamnaya, 0.1841) by a real, structural margin, and the purpose-built Caucasus-resolution model sharpens this into its clearest form yet — Armenian Chalcolithic Areni and Bronze Age Mycenaean Greece, both markers of the Aegean-Anatolian-South Caucasus Bronze Age corridor, rank closer to this genome than any other ancient population tested.

Beneath all of that, the Neolithic tier — Malak Preslavets and Krepost, two excavated Danube farming villages dated to roughly the 6th millennium BC — marks the deepest floor this genome still measurably connects to, and the archaic model's CHG-over-EHG weighting (Kotias closer than Karelia) identifies the specific southeastern flavour of the steppe ancestry that arrived on top of that farming substrate. The single-gene markers add one more thread: an EDAR variant usually tied to East Asian ancestry, present here without its usual genetic partner, is more consistent with an old, steppe-mediated route into this lineage than a recent one. Layer upon layer, the same conclusion holds: a genome assembled from Anatolian farmers, Caucasus-weighted steppe pastoralists, and a measurable, well-corroborated Aegean/South Caucasus Bronze Age thread running through the middle of it.

What remains open for a future update is narrower now: chromosome/cM-level detail on the archaic segment matches in the supplementary heat map, to turn the general Neanderthal-ancestry observation into a specific, located one.

In Plain Terms — The Whole Excavation, Start to Finish

Run the whole story in one direction, and it reads like peeling back layers of paint on an old house — each coat newer than the one beneath it, none of them erasing what came before. Start at the surface: a person whose closest living relatives are Bulgarian, Romanian, and Gagauz — solidly Eastern Balkan, no surprises. Peel back a few centuries and you reach the Slavic migrants who moved south through Hungary in the early Middle Ages and merged with whoever was already living in the Balkans — this genome sits unusually close to the ordinary population absorbed into that process, and just as unusually far from the steppe warlords who briefly ruled over it. Peel back further, past the Slavic layer, and you reach the people who were already there waiting: the Thracians and their Paleo-Balkan neighbours, farmers and hill-tribes who'd occupied this same stretch of land for thousands of years before any Slavic-speaker arrived.

Go back further still, past the Thracians, and the story stops being about named peoples and starts being about population-scale migrations. Around four thousand years ago, horse-riding herders from the Yamnaya culture swept out of the steppe north of the Black Sea and reshaped the genetics of most of Europe — this genome carries their signal too, weighted in a particular way (leaning toward the Caucasus Hunter-Gatherers, or CHG, rather than the Eastern Hunter-Gatherers, or EHG, who also fed into the Yamnaya) that points specifically toward the southeastern, Caucasus-adjacent branch of that migration rather than the northern one. Before the herders, there were the first farmers — people who left Anatolia, in what's now Turkey, around eight thousand years ago and slowly walked their crops and livestock into the Balkans, settling villages like the ones this genome's Neolithic matches come from. And before the farmers, this land belonged to hunter-gatherers who never farmed at all — a Western European branch (WHG) whose faint signal still shows up as the oldest, thinnest layer of paint on the wall, detectable but barely, in a direct segment match to an 8,000-year-old skeleton from Luxembourg.

And beneath even that: virtually everyone with non-African ancestry carries a small amount of Neanderthal DNA, and this genome is almost certainly no exception — the 0.5cM heat map shows a faint signal in the Vindija Cave fossils specifically, suggesting a distant thread of that same ancient lineage. Hunter-gatherers, then farmers, then steppe herders, then Thracians, then Slavic migrants, then medieval Bulgarians, then the living relatives found today, with a whisper of Neanderthal beneath all of it — each one a real, separately-confirmed layer in the same continuous excavation.
