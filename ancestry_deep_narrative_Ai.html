How to Read This Report Lower G25 distance = genetically closer. As a rough rule of thumb, distances under about 0.03 are very close matches and above about 0.1 the populations are clearly different. These scores measure similarity to reference populations, not “percent of your ancestors.” A distance cannot show descent, a migration route, or a date of contact, and similar coordinates can come from different mixtures. Modern labels (Japanese, Korean, Han) are names of present-day comparison groups, and ancient labels (Kofun, Gimhae, Yayoi, Jomon) are archaeological reference samples, not ancestors. What the Labels Are Telling Us, and What They Are Not

Every dig starts at the surface. Here the surface is the ethnicity estimate a standard DNA test hands back, and it is the least reliable part of the whole report. MyHeritage’s estimate for this kit:

Japanese (Japan, Okinawa)87.7%
Chinese12.3%

MyHeritage also lists four Additional Genetic Groups at lower confidence: two Southeast Asian groupings, Germany (Freiburg), and a group combining Indonesia, Singapore and Turkey. These are the names an algorithm attaches to very small signals. They are not evidence of ancestry from those places, and nothing in this report treats them that way.

“Japanese (Japan, Okinawa)” is the name of the reference group that Ai’s DNA matches best. It describes the panel the company built, not a family origin, and it does not by itself point to the Ryukyu Islands. Later layers test the Okinawa question properly. “Chinese (12.3%)” means part of her profile resembles the company’s reference panel for China. That is in line with the continental ancestry that modern Japanese populations carry generally, but it is not a count of Chinese ancestors, and it is measured against a different reference panel than the Global25 results later in this report.

The Bottom Line Read properly, the MyHeritage result says one thing: her DNA resembles Japanese reference samples far more than any other group, with a smaller continental-leaning share. The exact percentages belong to MyHeritage’s panel and would change with another tool.

Takeaway: The label is not the finding. Country names are modern reference tags, not a family tree.

The Okinawa Question

Ryukyuan people, the native inhabitants of Okinawa and the islands to its south, are known for carrying more ancestry from the old Jomon hunter-gatherers than mainland Japanese do. A large RIKEN-led study of 3,256 whole genomes (Liu et al. 2024) found Jomon-related ancestry highest in Okinawa, at about 28.5%, against about 19% in Tohoku and about 13% in Kansai. With “Okinawa” in the label of her MyHeritage reference group, it is natural to ask whether Ai’s DNA points south.

A modern Ryukyuan average was published in Global25 coordinates by ExploreYourDNA in October 2026, and it was compared with Ai. The raw distance is 0.048, just beyond the Han references (0.043–0.044) and well beyond Japanese (0.025). But the reference itself needs a look before the number means anything. This average sits 0.057 from the standard Japanese average, more than twice Ai’s own 0.025, and its nearest neighbors in the reference sheet are Han and Korean populations, not Japanese ones. That is the opposite of what a population carrying more Jomon ancestry than the mainland should look like. ExploreYourDNA’s own write-up calls the single Ryukyuan average thin evidence and prefers the 28.5% figure from the large genome study. One dimension in particular is odd: the last of the 25 numbers accounts for about 40% of the gap between this reference and the Japanese average, and differs from the Japanese average by roughly 4.7 times the spread seen among East Asian populations. The reference is labeled “low resolution”, and that fits, though the files do not say why.

So the 0.048 cannot be read as an Okinawan affinity, or as the lack of one. In every version of the test, including ones that leave out the oddest dimension, Ai sits closer to the mainland Japanese average (0.025, or 0.018 without the last dimension) than to the Ryukyuan reference (0.048, or 0.044). Imagine trying to measure a room with a ruler you suspect is slightly bent. You can still say which wall is closer, but not by how many centimeters. A proper test needs a better Ryukyuan reference, and, ideally, Ai’s own coordinate computed the same way as the references.

In Plain Terms All the tests agree on the headline: Ai’s DNA is Japanese, with Koreans as close neighbors. They do not agree on how much she leans toward China, and the one Okinawan reference available is too unusual to say anything about a southern connection. The next layers look at what history adds.

Takeaway: Closest to Japanese in every test, Korean a close second. The Okinawa comparison is inconclusive, because the Ryukyuan reference does not behave like a normal population average.

Historic Era
~250–1850 AD

Two shores of one strait, and an island to the south

Between the 3rd and 7th centuries, a new wave of people from the Asian mainland reached Japan, the age of the great keyhole-shaped burial mounds and of Japan’s first centralized state. A 2021 ancient-genome study found that people buried in that period already look almost exactly like modern Japanese. Ai’s DNA agrees, and adds a twist. Among the ancient samples in the reference sheet, three sit almost level with her: a Kofun-era individual from Honshu, and two people from Gimhae, in southern Korea, buried in the very same centuries. The measurements cannot say which is closest. What they say is that her DNA lands where Japan and southern Korea overlapped 1,700 years ago.

Far to the south, a separate set of coordinates adds a fascinating detail. Three people who lived on Miyako Island, in the southern Ryukyus, around 1800, are among the closest matches to Ai of any ancient or historic sample tested, the nearest of all at 0.017. They carry the same mainland-style neighborhood too, so for now this reads as a lead to follow, not a southern origin.

The Kofun Stratum: The Archipelago Settles

The Kofun period, from about the 3rd to the 7th century, takes its name from the enormous earthen burial mounds built across Japan, more than 160,000 of them. It was the age when a centralized state took shape and ideas, technology and people moved freely between Japan, Korea and China. The big genetic question is whether new people arrived too. The answer, from Cooke et al. (2021), is yes. They sequenced twelve ancient Japanese genomes, nine Jomon and three Kofun-period, and found that the older picture of two ancestral streams (Jomon and Yayoi farmers) was not enough. A third stream, East Asian ancestry resembling modern Han Chinese, arrived during the Kofun period. By their estimate it became the largest part of the mainland Japanese gene pool (press accounts cite about 71%), and Kofun-period individuals already look very much like modern Japanese, which suggests the mainland’s genetic make-up has been broadly stable for about 1,400 years.

Ai sits right there. On her coordinate, a Kofun-period sample from Honshu (Japan_Honshu_Kofun.SG, 0.022) is the nearest of any Japanese sample, ancient or modern, nearer than the modern Japanese average itself (0.025).

A Neighbor Across the Strait

The wider ancient sheet adds a neighbor. Two samples from Gimhae, in the Gaya region of southern Korea, are as close or closer: SouthKorea_Gimhae_DaeseongDong (0.018) and Gimhae_YuhaRi (0.023). They come from a 2022 study (Gelabert et al.) of eight genomes from the Gaya Confederacy, dated to roughly AD 300–500, which makes them contemporaries of the Kofun-period Japanese. That study found two genetic groups in Gimhae, one of them carrying Jomon-related ancestry, a hint that the people on the two sides of the Korea Strait were closely connected. The three closest samples span only 0.005, inside the margin of a simulated coordinate, and the Kofun and Daeseong-dong samples are only 0.016 apart themselves. So Ai’s DNA cannot be placed on the Japanese side of the strait rather than the Korean side. What can be said is that she sits in the genetic neighborhood the two shared in the 4th and 5th centuries.

The Southern Islands: Miyako Around 1800

The Ryukyu Islands stretch for a thousand kilometers between Kyushu and Taiwan, and their history differs from the mainland’s. Four people buried at the Nagabaka rock shelter on Miyako Island in the southern Ryukyus, who lived about 150 years ago, were sequenced by Robbeets et al. (2021). Cooke et al. (2023) then asked whether the same three-stream story holds there. It does, but with different proportions: about 26.7% Jomon, 30.5% Northeast Asian and 42.8% East Asian ancestry for the group, with wide margins of error, compared with about 13% Jomon in Kofun-period individuals and 15% in modern mainland Japanese. The islanders were not direct descendants of the Jomon, since two-stream models failed for them. The authors suggest the streams reached the southern Ryukyus together, in a later movement of people who already carried all three, who then mixed with the islands’ earlier, prehistoric inhabitants, in contrast to the mainland, where each stream arrived separately. These are group-level estimates from a formal method, and they say nothing yet about Ai.

A separate file of nine Nagabaka individuals (DNAGENICS coordinates) lets Ai be placed among them. They fall into two clear groups:

Does this point to the Ryukyus? It is an intriguing lead, but not yet a finding, for three reasons. First, the Nagabaka coordinates come from a different provider than the rest of this report, and providers can disagree materially (an earlier comparison of one individual across two providers found the two versions 0.167 apart), so these distances are kept within their own file and are not ranked against the Kofun and Gimhae numbers above. Second, NAG039 is one person. Third, as a rough diagnostic only, when the three nearest Nagabaka individuals are placed against the standard sheet, their closest neighbors are the Gimhae and Kofun samples (NAG039: Gimhae 0.026 and 0.028, Kofun 0.035). That is what you would expect for islanders who carry a large mainland-style East Asian share. It means Ai’s closeness to NAG039 may simply be the same neighborhood she shares with Kofun-era Japan and Gaya-era Korea, seen through a different person. The data in hand cannot tell the two readings apart.

One more oddity argues for caution. The five earlier-period coordinates do not line up with the sheet’s own Jomon-like Nagabaka sample from 2,800 years ago, which sits 0.16–0.22 from every individual in the nine-person file. Whatever the reason (different individuals, coverage or processing), their distances can only be read as “far”.

In Plain Terms The ancient people nearest Ai lived about 1,700 years ago, in Japan’s Kofun era and in the Gaya region of Korea across the strait. They are too close to each other for this test to pick a winner, and that fits what published studies found: the two shores were closely connected and Japan’s gene pool changed little afterwards. A separate set of Miyako Island people from around 1800 also sits very close to her, closest of all at one individual. Those islanders were themselves a mix of the same three streams that shaped mainland Japan, so being near one of them may mean Ai and he share a mainland-style background, not that her family came from the islands. It is worth testing properly. It is not yet a conclusion.

Takeaway: Kofun-era Japan, Gaya-era Korea (0.018–0.023) and historic Miyako individuals (0.017–0.037, separate coordinate set) all sit in Ai’s neighborhood. The Miyako link is a lead, not a conclusion.

Rice farmers on the front line

About 2,300 years ago, farmers from the continent reached northern Kyushu by way of the Korean peninsula, bringing wet-rice farming and bronze, and settled among the Jomon hunter-gatherers already living there. They did not sweep the Jomon away. For several centuries, on the same small islands, there were people who carried continental ancestry, people who carried none at all, and families mixing in between.

Four Yayoi-period individuals from the islands off northwest Kyushu are in the comparison. Two of them sit 0.063 and 0.090 from Ai, further than the Kofun samples, and two are as far from her as the Jomon themselves (about 0.165). That is what the history predicts: Ai’s DNA is dominated by the later, Kofun-era layer, not by the first farmers.

The Yayoi Stratum: Rice Farmers from the Continent

Rice farming reached the archipelago from the continent, through the Korean peninsula, and with it came people. The period is traditionally dated from about 300 BC, though radiocarbon dates place the start of continental gene flow earlier, around 2,500 years ago. Cooke et al. (2021) linked the Yayoi stream to Northeast Asian ancestry resembling ancient populations of the West Liao River region and the Amur basin. In their model the Northeast Asian stream dominates the continental ancestry of the Yayoi period, and the East Asian stream takes over in the Kofun period.

The Yayoi samples in the comparison come from northwest Kyushu, exactly where the first migrants would have landed: Shomura, on Iki Island (0.063), and individuals from the Neshiko site on Hirado Island. A 2026 study of four genomes from these islands (Kim et al., as summarized by ExploreYourDNA) found a striking picture: two Hirado individuals, Neshiko 13 and Neshiko 16, are essentially unmixed Jomon despite living in the farming era, while Neshiko 11, a century later, and the Shomura man carry substantial continental ancestry. Individual families were mixing in both directions. On Ai’s coordinate, Neshiko11 sits at 0.090, and the two Jomon-like Neshiko individuals at about 0.165, level with the Jomon samples.

The two Yayoi individuals who already carried continental ancestry were mixed with a lot of Jomon ancestry, and the later Kofun-era arrivals brought a different, more Han-like stream that dominates the modern profile. The people who matter most for her DNA arrived later, in the Kofun era, and mixed into a population that already contained the Yayoi and the Jomon. The Yayoi individuals measured here sit further from her than Kofun-era people do.

Takeaway: Yayoi-period individuals (0.063, 0.090) sit further from Ai than Kofun-era samples, as the three-stream history predicts. No Yayoi percentage can be given.

Yellow River
~2300–1800 BC

Millet farmers of the Central Plain: a clue that turned out to be a map

Four thousand years ago, along the middle Yellow River in China, farmers of the Longshan culture grew millet and lived in walled towns, in the region that later became the heart of Chinese civilization. Eight of them, from three sites, have had their DNA read and placed on the same map as Ai.

Ai sits closest to two of them, PLTM310 and WD-WT1H16, and the order of the eight is steady from one test to the next. That is tempting. But when the same eight are measured against Koreans, against Han Chinese, and against a Kofun-era Japanese, the order is the same. These two individuals are simply the ones nearest the middle of East Asia, and Ai is on that map like everyone else in the region. For now the Yellow River people read as a landmark, not as a thread leading to Ai.

Interesting, but not specific to Ai

The Dig

The Yellow River Stratum: Who Are the Eight?

The samples come from Ning et al. (2020), who analysed 55 ancient genomes from northern China covering the last 7,500 years. The Late Neolithic group of the Yellow River region (Longshan culture, roughly 2300–1800 BC) has eight individuals from three sites: two from Haojiatai, four from Pingliangtai and two from Wadian, matching the eight coordinate records used here. They are not a random sample of the people of the region, only the individuals who happened to be recovered and sequenced. The original paper found the Yellow River population to be broadly stable over time, with some input from southern-China-related groups since the Middle Neolithic. A 2023 study of the southern Ryukyus did not use this group as a source of Japanese ancestry; it used ancient Amur-related individuals and present-day Han Chinese. An earlier version of this report named the Yellow River Late Neolithic group as the nearest continental reference. With the eight individuals available it can be looked at properly, and the answer is more nuanced.

The Pattern, and What It Is

Two features stand out. First, the order is remarkably steady. In four earlier runs of Ai’s coordinate (derived from four different calculators), PLTM310 and WD-WT1H16 were always the two nearest, PLTM311 and PLTM313 always came next, PLTM312 was always fifth and HJTM107 always sixth. Second, the catalogue’s “inliers”, the individuals that sit with the group’s core, are on average closer to Ai than its “outliers”, the individuals flagged as atypical (an average of 0.053 against 0.066 here). The pattern is not perfectly tidy: the outlier PLTM312 ranks fifth, ahead of the inlier HJTM107. The four calculator runs are four ways of reading the same underlying genome, so their agreement shows the result is stable within this approach, not that it was independently reproduced.

The outliers are further for a straightforward reason. “Outlier” means the individual sits apart from the group’s core in the genetic map, and the outliers here are 0.032–0.040 from the group’s center, against 0.014–0.021 for the inliers. They are far from everyone, so being far from Ai says something about them, not about her. Individuals like these often reflect a mix of ancestries that differs from the group’s majority, which fits the picture in the original paper of a region in contact with neighbors to the north and south, though one person cannot confirm that. The catalogue’s labels were never meant to rank similarity to Ai.

Why It Is Not a Yellow River Thread

The key test is a simple one: does the same order appear for other groups? It does, almost exactly. Ranking the eight individuals by distance from modern Koreans, from a Kofun-period Japanese sample, from the Gimhae sample or from Han Chinese from Henan gives the same order as Ai’s (a rank correlation of 1.00 for Koreans, Kofun and Gimhae, 0.98 for Han Henan, 0.95 for the standard Japanese average). PLTM310 and WD-WT1H16 are simply the most central individuals of the eight, the nearest to the middle of the East Asian map for nearly everyone. What Can Be Said

Quite a lot, as long as it stays modest. The Yellow River farmers of 4,000 years ago are a snapshot of the northern part of the East Asian gene pool, from which later Han Chinese populations descend. Cooke et al. (2021) found that the Kofun-era arrivals in Japan resembled modern Han Chinese, so a Han-like continental element has long been part of Japan’s gene pool. Ai’s closeness to these individuals is consistent with that. It does not say that any of these people were her ancestors, that a Yellow River population moved to Japan, or that she has more Yellow River ancestry than other Japanese. No migration route can be read from it. Confidence in “Ai sits within the East Asian range, near the Yellow River group like every other East Asian population” is high; confidence in anything specific to the Yellow River is low.

In Plain Terms Imagine eight villagers standing in a field, and a ninth person walking in from the sea. Two of the villagers are standing nearest the middle of the field, so almost anyone walking in from any direction ends up closest to those two. That is what the Yellow River comparison shows. It tells you that Ai is on the East Asian map, near its middle, and not that she came from a particular village.

Takeaway: Ai is nearest PLTM310 and WD-WT1H16 (0.048–0.049), but so is every East Asian group, in the same order, and the pattern fades when the finest part of her coordinate is removed. The Yellow River is a useful landmark and not an ancestral source.

Layer 5
Jomon
~14,000–300 BC

The people who were already here

Before rice, Japan belonged to the Jomon, hunter-gatherer-fishers who lived in the archipelago for thousands of years, made some of the oldest pottery in the world, and stayed remarkably isolated. Every Jomon sample tested sits about equally far from Ai, as far as nearly any Japanese person sits from them, because the Jomon were a very distinctive population.

That distance does not mean she has no Jomon ancestry. Published estimates put the Jomon share at roughly 13–19% for mainland Japanese and higher in Okinawa, but a simple comparison with a simulated coordinate cannot measure it for one person, so no Jomon percentage is claimed here.

The Ryukyu islands tell the most striking Jomon story: on Miyako Island, people living 2,800 years ago were almost purely Jomon-like, while the people living there around 1800 were a mix of three streams.

Present in Japanese ancestry generally; not measurable for Ai with these data

The Dig

The Jomon Stratum: Fifteen Thousand Years Alone

The Jomon were hunter-gatherer-fishers who lived in the archipelago from about 16,000 years ago, and they made pottery from about 16,500 years ago, some of the oldest in the world. Their ancestors separated from other East Asians more than 15,000 years ago, and they stayed small and isolated for many thousands of years, which is why Jomon individuals who lived thousands of years apart, at opposite ends of the archipelago, look so alike genetically. Cooke et al. (2021) estimated about 13.1% Jomon ancestry in Kofun-period individuals and 15.0% in modern mainland Japanese. The RIKEN study (Liu et al. 2024) found the share ranges regionally, from about 13% in Kansai through about 19% in Tohoku to about 28.5% in Okinawa. Those are group estimates from formal methods and are not applied to Ai.

Seven Jomon-period samples were compared with Ai. All sit at 0.167–0.173, the same range as the standard Japanese average sits from the Jomon (0.165). The Jomon were so distinctive that nearly any modern Japanese person is about this far away.

The Southern Ryukyus Across 2,800 Years

Miyako Island gives a rare time series from a single place. The earliest Nagabaka sample, from about 2,800 years ago, is almost purely Jomon-like: an independent but non-peer-reviewed Global25 model by ExploreYourDNA puts two people buried there between about 800 and 680 BC at about 99% Jomon, as unmixed as mainland Jomon of two thousand years earlier. By about 1800 the picture had changed: Cooke et al. (2023) estimate about 27% Jomon, with continental ancestry accounting for the rest, and they conclude that the later arrivals were people who already carried the mainland streams. The estimate for modern Okinawa from the RIKEN study (about 28.5%) is similar in size to the 1800 value, though it concerns a different island and was made by a different method.

The standard sheet agrees with that picture in outline. The old Miyako sample sits 0.137 from the historic Miyako group and 0.171 from the standard Japanese average; the historic group sits 0.042 from Japanese and 0.040 from Kofun-period Honshu, and the Ryukyuan average sits 0.055 from the historic group and 0.167 from the old sample. In words: the southern islands changed from a Jomon-like population to a mainland-like one, and the modern Ryukyuan reference sits much nearer the 1800 state than the 2,800-year-old one. As elsewhere, the Ryukyuan average itself behaves oddly, so this is a rough guide to continuity, not a measurement of it.

Where does Ai fall? At 0.173 from the old Miyako sample and 0.040 from the historic group, she sits with the modern, mixed end of that sequence, with nothing to suggest a profile unusually close to the old Jomon-like islanders.

In Plain Terms The Jomon are the oldest people in this story who can still be measured, and Ai’s DNA is about as far from all of them as nearly everyone in Japan’s is. Whether she carries more or less Jomon ancestry than other people in Japan is a question for a proper test, not this one. On Miyako Island the Jomon lasted longest and then changed quickly, and Ai sits with the later, mixed state.

Takeaway: Every Jomon sample is about equally far (0.167–0.173), so no Jomon percentage is claimed. Miyako Island went from Jomon-like to mixed, and Ai sits with the mixed end.

The Dig

The Archaic Sediment: What Everyone Outside Africa Shares

Around 50,000–60,000 years ago, the ancestors of people outside Africa mixed with Neanderthals, and almost everyone outside Africa still carries a small share of that DNA, roughly 1–2% of the genome. On average East Asian genomes carry slightly more of it than European ones. A second archaic thread, from Denisovans, is present in East Asian populations at a much lower level, well under 1%, and far below what is seen in Oceanian populations. Neither was measured in Ai’s results, and nothing in them suggests a larger share than is typical.

In Plain Terms This layer is the same for almost everyone outside Africa, so it says little about where in Japan or Asia a person’s ancestors lived. It is on the map for completeness.

Takeaway: A small shared Neanderthal background and a fainter Denisovan trace are expected but unmeasured.

Notable
Findings

Three things worth a second look

The first is a neighborhood. Kofun-era Japan, Gaya-era Korea and historic Miyako Island all sit within a few hundredths of Ai’s coordinate. That is a real pattern, and it is the part of this report that holds up under every test, but it is a neighborhood, not an address.

The second is two clues that dissolved under testing. The Yellow River people and the Ryukyuan reference both looked, at first glance, like connections. Looked at closely, the first is a landmark every East Asian group is near, and the second is a reference that doesn’t behave like a normal population average.

The third is a lean. In the full coordinate Ai sits a little toward the Han end of the map, but most of that lean comes from one fine-scale dimension and fades when it is removed. It may be real, ordinary variation, or an artifact. It is not solved.

One solid pattern; two clues and a lean left open

The Dig

The Neighborhood That Holds Up

Ai’s results were re-run several ways: with all 25 dimensions of her coordinate, with the last (finest-scale) one removed, and with only the first ten (the broadest). Some findings survive every version. Japanese stays the nearest modern population, and the Gimhae and Kofun samples stay the nearest ancient ones (0.016 and 0.019 without the last dimension, 0.011 and 0.014 with only the first ten). The three nearest historic Miyako individuals barely move (NAG039 0.017, 0.017 and 0.015 across the three versions). Whatever else is uncertain about this coordinate, the neighborhood is not.

Takeaway: A Kofun–Gaya–Miyako neighborhood that survives every version of the test.

Two Clues That Dissolved

The Yellow River. Ai’s closeness to two Yellow River individuals looks like a pattern, and the stable order across four calculator runs makes it look like a result. But the same order appears for Koreans, Han and Kofun-era Japanese, so it describes the individuals, not Ai, and it reshuffles when the finest dimension is removed (Layer 4).

The Ryukyuan average. The reference sits 0.057 from the Japanese average, nearer to Han and Korean populations than to Japanese ones, although the Ryukyuan people carry more Jomon ancestry than the mainland does. Its last dimension alone accounts for about 40% of that gap. Ai’s 0.048 distance to it says nothing about a southern connection (Layer 1).

Takeaway: Both are worth revisiting with better references, and neither is a finding.

A Japanese genome, in a neighborhood that spans a strait

Hunter-gatherers who were here first, rice farmers who arrived from the continent, and a later wave of East Asian newcomers in the age of the burial mounds, layered together and mixed. Ai’s DNA is not one people. It is the meeting place of several, which is how Japan itself was made.

The Dig

The Complete Excavation: What All Seven Layers Tell Together

At the surface, MyHeritage describes Ai as Japanese with a smaller Chinese share, labels that say more about its reference panel than about her family tree. The Global25 distances are more precise: Japanese at 0.025, Korean close behind at 0.028, Manchu and Han further out. Five calculators agree on Japanese first, and the order held in every version of the test. The Okinawan reference added in this edition is too unusual to say anything about a southern connection.

Beneath the modern layer the published history of the archipelago supplies the structure: Jomon hunter-gatherers, Yayoi farmers from Northeast Asia, and a Kofun-era influx of East Asian ancestry resembling modern Han Chinese. The ancient samples fit that order. The nearest are a trio from about 1,700 years ago, Gimhae in Gaya-era Korea (0.018), Kofun-period Honshu (0.022) and a second Gimhae sample (0.023), and their closeness to each other fits the picture of two shores in close contact. Historic individuals from Miyako Island in the southern Ryukyus also sit close (0.017–0.037, a separate coordinate set), mixed in their ancestry and with more Jomon than the mainland, which makes them a lead for future testing, not a southern origin. The Yayoi samples (0.063, 0.090) follow, the Yellow River farmers of 4,000 years ago (0.048–0.071) are a landmark that every East Asian group sits near, in the same order, and the Jomon are furthest (0.167–0.173), as they are for almost everyone in Japan. Eleven direct genotypes all fit East Asian ancestry.

The measurements cannot yet put numbers on the layers: the Yayoi individuals come from one corner of Kyushu, the coordinate behind the distances is simulated, and the finest dimension of it is the least reliable. What survives every version of the test is the neighborhood.

In Plain Terms: The Whole Excavation, Start to Finish

Run the story from the top down and it reads like peeling paint off an old house. Each coat is newer than the one beneath it, and none has erased the others. At the surface is a woman whose closest living relatives are Japanese, with Koreans close behind. Peel back a little and there are the Kofun years, when the burial mounds went up and new people arrived from the mainland, and across the strait the Gaya people of Korea, who look almost the same in this test. Peel back further and there are the Yayoi rice farmers who had come before, mixing with the people already there.

Beneath them are the Jomon, who lived in the islands for thousands of years in near isolation, and whose last unmixed descendants may have been on islands like Miyako, until newcomers reached them too. Beneath even that, as for almost everyone outside Africa, is a faint trace of Neanderthal ancestry. Each layer is real history, and Ai’s DNA places her within the Japanese population in the neighborhood those layers built.

Takeaway: One continuous story: Jomon → Yayoi farmers → Kofun-era newcomers → the modern Japanese population, with Korea and the southern islands as close neighbors. The Yellow River and Ryukyu connections are open questions, not findings.

What Would Settle the Open Questions A genuine Global25 coordinate for Ai, computed from her raw file through the same pipeline as the reference sheet, would show how much of the finest-dimension signal survives, and would let the Yellow River, Ryukyuan and Nagabaka comparisons be made properly.
