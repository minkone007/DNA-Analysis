Seven layers follow, each deeper than the last. Before descending into them, here is what the whole excavation shows, top to bottom.

Ai’s genome sits inside the Japanese reference population in every comparison made. On her simulated Global25 coordinate the closest modern population is Japanese (0.025), followed by Korean (0.028) and Korean_Antu (0.029). That is consistent in direction with the continental ancestry present in modern Japanese populations generally, but it is not a count of Chinese ancestors, and it is measured against a different reference panel than the Global25 results later in this report.

The Bottom Line Read properly, the MyHeritage result says one thing: her DNA resembles Japanese reference samples far more than any other group, with a smaller continental-leaning share. The exact percentages belong to MyHeritage’s panel and would change with another tool.

The Kofun to Heian Stratum — The Archipelago Settles

Between about the 3rd and 7th centuries, the Kofun period, Japan’s first centralized state took shape, and the Heian period carried that world forward to the end of the 12th century. The big genetic question is whether new people arrived. A 2021 ancient-genome study of Japan (Cooke et al.) found they did: a later influx of East Asian ancestry in the Kofun period, on top of the Jomon and Yayoi layers. The same study found that Kofun-period individuals and modern Japanese are almost genetically indistinguishable, which suggests the genetic makeup of the archipelago has been broadly stable for about 1,400 years.

Ai’s results agree. A Kofun-period Honshu sample, Japan_Honshu_Kofun.SG, is the closest sample of any period in the ancient sheet: 0.022 on the consensus coordinate, and 0.019–0.022 across the four weightings. It is nearer than the modern Japanese average in all four weightings, but only by 0.001–0.004, which is within the noise of a simulated coordinate, so the safe reading is that Ai sits level with the Kofun-period sample. The next-closest sample is Japan_Nagabaka_historic at 0.040, a historic-period population from Miyako Island in the southern Ryukyus, then a long gap to the Jomon samples at 0.167 and beyond. Because Nagabaka is on Miyako Island, it is also the only Ryukyu-linked reference that can be compared. Ai sits 0.040 from it against 0.022 from Kofun-period Honshu, so these results do not point to a closer link with the southern Ryukyus than with the Honshu Kofun population. With one historic population as the reference this is a weak test of the MyHeritage “Okinawa” label, not a verdict. A mixture of modern Japanese and Nagabaka_historic (about 79% and 21%, within a point of that in all four weightings) improves the fit by only about 0.002, against 0.007–0.009 for Japanese plus Han (Shanxi). Ai’s offset from the modern Japanese average therefore points more toward the mainland than toward the southern Ryukyus.

The Yayoi Stratum — Rice Farmers from the Continent

Rice farming reached the archipelago from the continent, through the Korean peninsula, and with it came people. The period is traditionally dated from about 300 BC, though some radiocarbon work places its start earlier. Unlike many places where incoming farmers replaced local hunter-gatherers, the Yayoi newcomers appear to have mixed with the Jomon population already living there.

Yayoi-period samples are now part of the comparison: two individuals from northwest Kyushu (Nagasaki Prefecture), dated to roughly 2,300–2,500 years ago, from the Shomura and Neshiko sites. They are Shomura (0.063) and Neshiko11 (0.090) from Ai’s coordinate. Two further Neshiko individuals are strongly Jomon-like and sit about 0.165 away, at the level of the Jomon samples. This northwest Kyushu group is a Jomon-leaning branch of the Yayoi and is not representative of every Yayoi migrant population, so continental stand-ins are kept alongside them: China_YR_LN (0.055) and Korea_Changhang (0.100). The Kofun sample (0.022) is still the nearest of all.

They are further from Ai than the Kofun-era sample, whose ancestry already combines the Yayoi and Jomon layers and describes her better than any Yayoi sample or combination of stand-ins does.

Takeaway: Yayoi-period samples (0.063, 0.090) and continental stand-ins are all further away than the Kofun sample, as expected. No Yayoi percentage can be given.

Before rice, the archipelago was home to the Jomon, hunter-gatherer-fishers who lived there for thousands of years in relative isolation. Seven Jomon-period samples were tested, and every one is far from Ai’s coordinate, at 0.167–0.173. That is normal. The Jomon were a very distinct population, and modern Japanese are mostly something else, so a large distance does not mean there is no Jomon ancestry.

The Earlier Jomon Stratum — Can the Phases Be Told Apart?

The Jomon period runs for many thousands of years, from about 16,000 years ago to somewhere between 3,000 and 2,300 years ago, depending on how the transition to farming is dated. The samples in Layer 4 span that range: Initial and Early Jomon (0.173 and 0.169) against Middle–Late and Late Jomon (0.171 and 0.172). The spread across all seven is only 0.007, smaller than the shifts caused by simply changing the weighting of the source coordinates, so there is no consistent difference between earlier and later Jomon in these results.

This layer is therefore kept on the map as a placeholder for finer resolution, not as a finding. Separating earlier from later Jomon ancestry would need a genuine Global25 coordinate, and probably a model built specifically for the Jomon phases.

In Plain Terms Earlier and later Jomon samples are all equally far from Ai, so these results cannot say which phase she is closer to, or whether the question is even meaningful at this resolution.

Takeaway: No separation between Early and Late Jomon in these results. Needs a genuine G25 coordinate.

The Archaic Sediment — What Everyone Outside Africa Shares

Beneath the Jomon sits the deepest layer, which is shared. Around 50,000–60,000 years ago, ancestors of people outside Africa mixed with Neanderthals, and almost everyone outside Africa still carries a small share of that DNA, about 1–4% of the genome. On average East Asian genomes carry somewhat more of it than European ones. Ai almost certainly carries it too, but it has not been measured in her results, and no segment-level test was run.

The map also holds a faint Denisovan-related thread, taken from Julian’s analysis, where it was inferred from Onge-related ancestry and attributed to his maternal side. It is carried here as a hypothesis only. Onge was not run for Ai, so nothing in her results supports it or rules it out.

In Plain Terms This layer is the same for almost everyone outside Africa, so it says little about where in Japan or Asia a person’s ancestors lived. It is on the map for completeness, not as a finding about Ai.

Takeaway: A small shared Neanderthal background is expected but unmeasured. The Denisovan thread is a hypothesis carried over from Julian’s analysis.

Small recurring Oceanian signals

Two calculators report a small Papuan or Oceanian component: HarappaWorld 1.14% and Eurogenes K13 1.71% (Oceanian). Low components like these are often within noise and can reflect deep ancestry shared with Southeast Asian lineages. They are not treated as evidence of Papuan or Denisovan ancestry, and the Denisovan thread on the map does not rely on them.

Takeaway: Noted, not interpreted.

The Complete Excavation — What All Seven Layers Tell Together

At the surface, MyHeritage describes Ai as Japanese with a smaller Chinese share, labels that say more about its reference panel than about her family tree. The Global25 distances cut through the labelling: her coordinate is nearest to Japanese (0.025), with Korean close behind (0.028) and the Han references clearly further. Beneath the modern layer, the history of the archipelago supplies the structure: a Kofun-era East Asian influx on top of Yayoi continental farmers, who themselves mixed with the Jomon population already living there. The ancient samples fit that story in order. The nearest of all is a Kofun-period Honshu sample (0.022), level with the modern Japanese average; the continental farming references follow (China YR LN 0.055, Korea Changhang 0.100), and the Jomon samples are furthest (0.167–0.173) with no consistent difference between earlier and later phases. Eight direct genotypes all point to East Asian ancestry. The measurements cannot yet put numbers on the layers: the Yayoi-period samples come from one Jomon-leaning region, the Jomon share in the exploratory model is a model output, and the coordinate behind the distances is simulated.

In Plain Terms — The Whole Excavation, Start to Finish

Start at the surface: a person whose closest living relatives are Japanese, with Koreans close behind. Peel back a little and there is the Kofun era, when a further wave of East Asian ancestry arrived. Peel back further and there are the Yayoi rice farmers, who came from the continent and mixed with the people already there. Beneath them are the Jomon, hunter-gatherer-fishers who had the islands to themselves for thousands of years.

Beneath even that, as for almost everyone outside Africa, is a small shared slice of Neanderthal DNA. Each layer is real history. Ai’s DNA places her within the Japanese population and fits that history in direction. What it cannot yet do is say how much of each layer she carries.

Takeaway: A Japanese genome that follows the known layers of Japanese history. The proportions need better references and a genuine Global25 coordinate.
