What the Labels Are Telling You — and What They Are Not

Every ancestry report begins with percentages and place names. Julian's MyHeritage results read: Japanese 61.6% (Okinawa), Balkan 21.2%, Greek and Albanian 10.8%, East European 3.9%, Central Asian 2.5%. A natural reading of these numbers suggests a person who is part Japanese, part Balkan, part Greek — as though different ancestral populations contributed separate, countable slices. That reading is incorrect, and correcting it is where the real story begins.

These percentages are not ancestry fractions in the way a family tree would show them. They are the output of an algorithm comparing Julian's DNA against modern reference populations and asking: which living groups does he most resemble, and in what proportions? The answer is shaped by which reference populations the algorithm has access to, how those populations are defined, and which genetic signals are most diagnostic. The same genome, run through a different algorithm with a different reference panel, will produce different percentages — as the GEDmatch calculators in this report demonstrate repeatedly.

What the MyHeritage result is genuinely telling us, beneath the percentage labels, is this: Julian's genome contains two major ancestry streams that have almost no overlap with each other. One stream traces to the Eastern Balkans — the genetic world of modern Bulgarians, Romanians, and Greeks, itself a layered mixture of ancient European farmers, Bronze Age steppe pastoralists, and indigenous Balkan populations. The other stream traces to the Ryukyuan islands of southern Japan — a world defined by one of the most genetically isolated human populations ever identified, the Jomon hunter-gatherers of the Japanese archipelago.

The Okinawa flag is the most important detail in the entire report. MyHeritage does not flag regional subgroups casually. Okinawa and the Ryukyu archipelago are genetically distinct from mainland Japan — not merely culturally. Mainland Japanese carry approximately 10–15% ancestry from the ancient Jomon hunter-gatherers who originally inhabited the islands; the rest is from the Yayoi farming populations who migrated from the Korean peninsula roughly 2,300 years ago. Okinawans carry up to 40% Jomon ancestry, because the Yayoi expansion reached the Ryukyus later and less completely. When the algorithm flags Okinawa, it is detecting a Jomon signal in Julian's genome that is stronger than would be expected from a mainland Japanese ancestry. His mother's family carries a disproportionately large share of one of the oldest surviving genetic legacies in East Asia.

The Central Asian 2.5% is not an error and not a separate ancestral population. It appears on both sides of Julian's genome — as a trace signal on his paternal Balkan side through deep Bronze Age steppe connections, and as a larger signal on his maternal East Asian side through the northeastern Asian component of his mother's ancestry. Its source becomes clear as we dig deeper.

Why the Numbers Differ Across Calculators Julian's results from different GEDmatch calculators appear to contradict each other: one says 40% East Asian, another says 32%, another 29%. None of them is wrong. Each calculator uses a different set of reference populations and measures different genetic dimensions. What matters is not the precise percentage from any single tool, but what is consistent across all of them. Across every calculator run on Julian's DNA, two things remain constant: a strong Eastern Balkan European signal, and a strong East Asian signal that clusters closer to Jomon-related populations than to mainland Han Chinese. Everything else is a question of how finely each tool can resolve the picture.

The Mesolithic Stratum — Two Hunter-Gatherer Worlds

Beneath the farming layers on both sides of Julian's ancestry, we reach the oldest surviving human populations in Europe and East Asia: the hunter-gatherers who preceded the agricultural revolution entirely, and whose genetic legacies were partially but never fully overwritten by the farming expansions that followed.

On the paternal side, the Western Hunter-Gatherers — post-Ice Age forest people who roamed Europe for thousands of years before the first farmers arrived — contribute approximately 15% of Julian's father's genome, arriving to Julian at roughly half that proportion after maternal dilution. Luxembourg Loschbour ranks at distance 0.425 in Julian's ancient DNA results, the most distant of the major ancient populations tested, its signal present but mathematically overshadowed by the much larger East Asian component that dominates his overall coordinates. The WHG legacy is nonetheless real and visible in specific genetic markers — most notably in the HERC2 blue-eye haplotype, which Julian inherited from his father. Ancient DNA from Mesolithic European hunter-gatherers, including La Braña in Spain and Loschbour in Luxembourg, consistently shows the blue-eye variant at high frequency. It is one of the oldest traits in Julian's phenotype, older than fair skin, older than lactose tolerance, predating agriculture in Europe entirely.

On the maternal side, the Jomon people of the Japanese archipelago occupy an equivalent position — ancient, pre-agricultural, genetically isolated, and preserved most completely in the populations that received the least subsequent admixture. Japan HG Jomon ranks at distance 0.407 in Julian's ancient DNA results. The gap between this distance and the Han Henan distance of 0.478 — a 17% difference — places Julian's East Asian ancestry unmistakably closer to Jomon hunter-gatherers than to any continental East Asian farming population. This is the quantitative confirmation of what the MyHeritage Okinawa flag suggests: the East Asian component of his genome is Jomon-proximate, not Han-proximate.

The Jomon arrived in the Japanese archipelago approximately 30,000–16,000 years ago, during periods when lower sea levels permitted land crossings from the Asian mainland. Once the seas rose, they were isolated for millennia, developing a genetic profile found nowhere else on earth. They were maritime hunters, fishers, and shell-mound builders — makers of the world's oldest fired pottery. Ancient DNA shows they carried dark skin, wavy or curly hair, and double eyelids, a phenotype substantially different from modern mainland East Asians, reflecting their long isolation from the continental populations that would eventually produce the Yayoi farmers.

Two Hunter-Gatherer Worlds, One Genome Western Hunter-Gatherers and Jomon hunter-gatherers last shared a common ancestor approximately 40,000–50,000 years ago, when the first anatomically modern humans spread out of Africa and began differentiating into the populations that would eventually inhabit opposite ends of the Eurasian continent. They evolved in complete genetic isolation for tens of thousands of years — the WHG in the forests and plains of Ice Age Europe, the Jomon ancestors on the Pacific fringe of Asia. Neither population knew the other existed. Julian's genome brings them together for the first time, the one contributing the blue-eye allele on chromosome 15, the other the deep Pacific ancestry on the opposing strand, both arriving intact through parents whose own ancestry had never previously intersected.

The Archaic Sediment — Neanderthals, Denisovans, and the Oldest Inheritance

At the deepest layer of the excavation, beneath all the historical migrations and prehistoric population movements, lie the oldest genetic threads in Julian's genome: DNA inherited not from modern humans at all, but from two other human species that our ancestors encountered and interbred with tens of thousands of years ago.

The Neanderthal Inheritance

Julian's genome contains Neanderthal DNA — as does every person of non-African descent, who carries approximately 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry from interbreeding between modern humans and Neanderthals in western Asia approximately 50,000–60,000 years ago. His GEDmatch chromosome painting includes Neanderthal reference genomes in its top rows, and it is worth being precise about what the orange segments in those rows actually mean. Each orange block indicates that a Neanderthal reference genome shares alleles with Julian in that chromosomal region. This is a measure of allele overlap, not a claim of direct descent from any specific Neanderthal individual.

With that in mind, the painting shows clear and repeated allele sharing with Vindija Cave (Croatia, ~40,000–44,000 years old) — scattered matches across multiple chromosomes. This is entirely expected and unremarkable in the sense that Vindija is the highest-quality European Neanderthal genome sequenced to date and serves as the primary reference for Neanderthal introgression studies worldwide. Modern Eurasians generally show the greatest detectable allele overlap with Vindija-like populations; Julian's Vindija signal reflects this shared human inheritance, not a specific Croatian Neanderthal ancestor. A weaker but present signal also appears against Mezmaiskaya (Adygia, Russia, ~29,000 years old), a Caucasus Neanderthal genome. Again, this reflects allele sharing consistent with Neanderthal introgression broadly, not descent from this individual.

What the chromosome painting reveals far more powerfully is everything that follows the Neanderthal rows. The orange bands expand dramatically and continuously through the Western Hunter-Gatherer, Anatolian Neolithic farmer, Bell Beaker, Yamnaya steppe, and European Bronze Age reference genomes on the paternal side — and through Jomon, Ainu, and Northeast Asian reference genomes further down. This overwhelming signal from the normal West Eurasian population sequence and from Jomon-related East Asian populations is the true headline of the painting. It is a visual confirmation of exactly what the G25 models found: Julian's genome is coherently and completely explained by the archaeological excavation already described — WHG and farmers and steppe on the paternal side, Jomon and Northeast Asian on the maternal side — with the Neanderthal rows at the top representing the oldest and most universal human inheritance that both lineages share.

Julian inherits Neanderthal-introgressed segments from both parents. His father carries a homozygous GG genotype at rs12041331 in the CLEC2D region of Chromosome 1, matching the Vindija Cave Neanderthal reference — the Neanderthal-derived allele inherited from both of his own parents. These introgressed Neanderthal variants, accumulated across the genome from both parental lineages, most commonly affect keratin and skin function, immune response, and blood coagulation — adaptations that proved useful when modern humans entered environments where Neanderthals had lived for hundreds of thousands of years.

Unlike Neanderthal admixture, which is spread relatively evenly across all non-African populations, Denisovan admixture is geographically skewed. Mainland East Asians carry approximately 0.2% Denisovan ancestry. The Jomon people, whose ancestry forms a major part of Julian's maternal genome, carried Onge-related ancestry inherited from ancient Southeast Asian hunter-gatherers who had previously encountered and interbred with Denisovans during their long southward migration through Southeast Asia.

Where the Two Worlds Are Readable at a Single Base Pair

The preceding layers have described Julian's ancestry through population statistics, ancient DNA distances, and historical narrative. The final layer of evidence is the most direct: specific positions in his own DNA where the two ancestry streams are visible side by side, encoded in a single nucleotide.

At position rs17822931 in the ABCC11 gene, Julian's genotype is TC — heterozygous. His father carries CC at this position: wet earwax, the ancestral Western Eurasian variant, found at near-100% frequency in European populations. His mother almost certainly carries TT: dry earwax, the derived East Asian variant, found at near-100% frequency in Japanese, Korean, and Chinese populations, and nearly absent in Europeans. The T allele is part of a coordinated selective sweep that swept through East Asian populations approximately 30,000 years ago, affecting hair follicle thickness, sweat gland density, and earwax type simultaneously as a linked genetic unit. Julian's TC genotype means he received the Western Eurasian C from his father's Eastern Balkan lineage and the East Asian T from his mother's Ryukyuan heritage. At a single position in his genome, both ancestries are legible, one letter each.

The eye colour and pigmentation loci tell the same story. His HERC2 GA genotype at the primary eye colour switch combines his father's GG — the blue-eye haplotype, inherited ultimately from the Mesolithic Western Hunter-Gatherers who carried it at high frequency — with his mother's likely A allele, the ancestral dark-eye variant. Julian probably has hazel or intermediate eye colour: the oldest eye colour variant in Europe meeting the oldest eye colour variant in East Asia, heterozygous in one individual. His SLC45A2 CG genotype follows the same structure — his father's G allele, the European light-skin variant that spread with the Neolithic farmers, paired with the ancestral C allele from his mother's ancestry. Intermediate pigmentation is the expected result, and it is the literal average of two genetic worlds that diverged 40,000 years ago.

The Complete Excavation — What All Eight Layers Tell Together

The excavation is complete. Eight layers of time have been removed, and what they reveal is not a patchwork of separate ancestries but a single coherent story of how two long-separated human lineages came to meet in one person.

At the surface, Julian's genome registers most strongly as Bulgarian — the closest modern population at distance 0.126, the paternal anchor that remains the dominant signal even across 61% East Asian admixture. Beneath that surface, the medieval layer reveals the two streams that built the Bulgarian genetic profile: a Paleo-Balkan substrate of approximately 48% — the Thracians, Dacians, Illyrians, and Romanised Balkan populations already present before the migrations — and a Slavic migration contribution of approximately 32%, arriving through the Pannonian corridor already mixed with Central European elements. The closest single ancient match in the entire database — Mongolia Arkhangai Early Medieval at distance 0.094 — sits in this same medieval stratum, a finding the data presents without requiring resolution.
